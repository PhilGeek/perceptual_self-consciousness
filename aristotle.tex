\chapter{Perceiving that We See and Hear} % (fold)
\label{cha:perceiving}

\section{Introduction} % (fold)
\label{sec:introduction2}

We perceive that we see and hear (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b12). Aristotle does not establish this so much as he presupposes it. In so doing, he poses a how-possible question. Given that we see and hear, how is it possible that we do? How-possible questions are not posed out of the blue. If they are apt, then what occasions the question is a doubt or unclarity about that very possibility. (On how-possible questions see \citealt{Cassam:2007lq}.) What occasions Aristotle's how-possible question is the aporetic doubt about reflexive intentional powers developed in the \emph{Charmides}. Or so shall I argue. For now, observe that there are at least two major points of contact. 

% First, like Socrates (not only in the \emph{Charmides} but also in the \emph{Gorgias}, for a recent comparison see \citealt[chapter 2]{Moss:2021aa}), Aristotle maintains that the objects of perception are transcendent:
% \begin{quote}
% 	For perception is surely not the perception of itself, but there is something beyond the perception, which must be prior to the perception. For that which moves is prior in nature to that which is moved. (Aristotle, \emph{Metaphysica} {\sbl Γ} 1010b 35-6)
% \end{quote}
% What is perceived is distinct from the perceiving of it. In Sartre's terminology, the intentional object is transcendent in the sense that it goes beyond the conscious act directed upon it. For Aristotle, in the case of perception, this follows from the causal role of the object of perception which acts upon the perceiver's sense organ and so must be distinct and prior in nature to that perception. Aristotle, here, makes explicit the tension between intentionality and reflexivity first raised in the \emph{Charmides}. If the object of perception is transcendent, then perception cannot be of itself, at least if that involves reflexive intentionality.
%
% 

First, in what \citet{caston02} dubs \textsc{The Duplication Argument} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b13–15), Aristotle claims that a perceiving of a seeing would perceive as well the object seen. Why this should be so has puzzled some commentators (\citealt[435]{Hicks:1907uq}, \citealt[121–2]{Hamlyn:2002ys}, and \citealt[44–5]{Kosman:2014ab}). Such puzzlement is abated if this claim is recognized as an instance of transitivity (\citealt{McCabe:2007ss}, \citealt{McCabe:2007jb}) or transparency (\citealt{Tsouna:2022aa}) whose denial was itself criticized in the \emph{Charmides} (most explicitly in \textsc{The Argument from Benefit}, \emph{Charmides} 170a–175b, but doubts were raised as early as \textsc{The Puzzling Disanalogies}, \emph{Charmides} 167c–168a).

Second, Aristotle raises a difficulty based on the claim that seeing is of color or of colored things (\emph{De anima} 425b17–9). Given this, if perceiving that we see is a seeing that we see, then the seeing that is seen must itself be colored. Again, why this should be so has puzzled some commentators (\citealt[122]{Hamlyn:2002ys}, \citealt[45–6]{Kosman:2014ab}). And, again, such puzzlement is abated if this claim is recognized as an instance of a key premise from \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} (\emph{Charmides} 168b2–169c2). Specifically, it is an instance of the claim that in order for an psychic power to apply to its object that object must have a certain nature of being (\emph{ousia}), color or being colored in the case of sight (\emph{Charmides} 168d1–5).

When Socrates, in the \emph{Charmides} (169a1–7), declares that a Great Man (\emph{megalou} \ldots \emph{andros}) is wanted to employ the method of division to distinguish those reflexive powers that are impossible from those that are possible, Plato, through Socrates, has issued an Academic Call For Papers. Aristotle can be read as responding to that call, even if the task of the Great Man is accomplished only in part. The reasoning of the opening \emph{aporia} deploys a recognizable descendent of the method of division. But Aristotle does not here consider the possibility of every reflexive psychic power, let alone reflexive natural powers (though see the discussion of spontaneous combustion in \emph{De anima} 2.5 417a). So the task of the Great Man is only partially accomplished, though Aristotle may be understood to undertake aspects of that larger work in other places such as in his discussion of relatives (\emph{Categoriae} 7, \emph{Topica} 4.6, \emph{Metaphysica} {\sbl Δ} 15) and powers more generally (\emph{De anima} 2.5, \emph{Metaphysica} {\sbl Θ} 9).

\section{The Opening \emph{Aporia}} % (fold)
\label{sec:the_opening_aporia}

\emph{De Anima} 3.2 opens and closes with \emph{aporiai}. Whereas the opening \emph{aporia} concerns perceptual self-consciousness, the closing \emph{aporia} concerns apperceptive unity. Spe\-cifically the opening \emph{aporia} concerns perceiving that we see and hear, and the closing \emph{aporia} concerns perceiving that something is white and sweet. Whiteness and sweetness are the objects of different senses—vision and taste respectively—and yet we experience these as bound to the same object. The \emph{aporiai} about perceptual self-consciousness and apperceptive unity are linked in \emph{De somno et vigilia} 2 455a12ff by admitting of a common solution. Perhaps, the opening and closing \emph{aporiai} of \emph{De Anima} 3.2 are related as well. We shall return to this question in section~\ref{sec:apperceptive_unity}.

\subsection{A Schematic Representation} % (fold)
\label{sub:a_schematic_representation}

It will be useful to have some overall sense of the opening \emph{aporia}. Consider, then, the following schematic representation of the aporetic reasoning:

\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
	\item \textsc{The How-Possible Question}: Since we perceive that we see and hear, it must either be by (a) \emph{opsis} that one perceives that one sees or (b) some other [\emph{aisthēsis}]. (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b12–3)
	\item \textsc{The Duplication Argument}: But the same [\emph{aisthēsis}] will be of \emph{opsis} and the underlying color. So either (a) there will be two [\emph{aisthēseis}] of the same, or (b) the same [\emph{aisthēsis}] will be of itself. (\emph{De anima} 3.2 4225b13–15)
	\item \textsc{The Regress Argument}: Further, if the \emph{aisthēsis} which is of \emph{opsis} is different, either (a) this will go on to infinity or (b) some \emph{aisthēsis} will be of itself. So one should grant this of the first. (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b15-7)
	\item \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}: But there is a puzzle (\emph{aporia}). For if to perceive by \emph{opsis} is to see, and if it is color or what has color that is seen, then if one is to see that which sees (\emph{to horōn}), that which sees (\emph{to horōn}) must have color in the primary way. (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b17–20)
	\item \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light}: But it is clear that to perceive by \emph{opsis} is no single thing; for even when we do not see, it is by \emph{opsis} that we discriminate dark and light, though not in the same way. (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b20–2)
	\item \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery}: Further, that which sees (\emph{to horōn}) is in a sense colored. For each sense organ (\emph{aisthētērion}) receives the sense object without its matter; that is why even after the sense objects have gone, sensations and images remain in the sense organ (\emph{aisthētēríos}). (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b22–5)
\end{enumerate} 

Though a close paraphrase of the opening \emph{aporia}, part of what makes the representation schematic is that it leaves key terms whose interpretation is contested untranslated. As a consequence, the schematic representation does not by itself determine the overall structure of the aporetic reasoning.

Consider \emph{opsis}, a persistent thread with which the \emph{aporia} is woven, occurring as it does in steps (1)–(5) of the schematic representation. There are three available readings:
\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
	\item \textsc{The Power Reading}: \emph{Opsis} is the power of sight (\citealt{Johansen:2005hz}).
	\item \textsc{The Activity Reading}: \emph{Opsis} is the activity of sight, the characteristic exercise of that power, seeing. (\citealt{caston02}).
	\item \textsc{The Corporeal Reading}: \emph{Opsis} is the corporeal instrument of sight, the sense organ, in this case, the eyes (\citealt{Trubowitz:2025aa}).
\end{enumerate}
\textsc{The Power Reading} is perhaps the most natural, independent of context. In this regard, the English is like the Greek where, independent of context, ``sight'' is most naturally understood as a perceptual power, the power to see. But as \citet{caston02} argues, \emph{opsis} may, in addition, be used to designate, not a perceptual power, but the activity that is its exercise. Again, and in this regard, the English is like the Greek, ``sight'' may designate, less the power to see, but the seeing of something. But \emph{opsis} can also be understood as designating, not the power, nor its characteristic exercise, seeing, but the corporal instrument, or sense organ, involved in the power's exercise, the eyes (\citealt[]{Bonitz:1870aa}, \citealt[]{Hicks:1907uq}, and \citealt{Trubowitz:2025aa}). There is no corresponding understanding of ``sight'' in English. For what it is worth, none is attested in the OED. Importantly, \emph{opsis} might not admit of a univocal interpretation throughout our passage. Given its context, one occurrence might only admit of the power reading, say, while another the activity or corporeal reading. So our interpretative choices, here, potentially affect our understanding of the overall structure of the aporetic reasoning.

Similarly, \emph{aisthēsis} admits of multiple readings along multiple dimensions. Along a first dimension, \emph{aisthēsis} may be either the power of perception or its exercise:
\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
	\item \textsc{The Power Reading}: \emph{Aisthēsis} is the power of perception.
	\item \textsc{The Activity Reading}: \emph{Aisthēsis} is the activity of the power of perception, the characteristic exercise of that power, perceiving.
\end{enumerate}
Along a second dimension, \emph{aisthēsis} may be either perceptual awareness more narrowly or awareness more generally:
\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
	\item \textsc{The Perceptual Reading}: \emph{Aisthēsis} is specifically perceptual awareness.
	\item \textsc{The Nonperceptual Reading}: \emph{Aisthēsis} is awareness more generally.
\end{enumerate}
These are not the only dimensions of semantic difference. For example. \emph{aisthēsis} may be sensory experience more broadly construed than perception, such as bodily sensation. However, as \textsc{The How Possible Question} is specifically about perception, we can safely ignore this contrast.

Moreover, these two dimensions may interact. So \textsc{The Power Reading} may be combined with either \textsc{The Perceptual Reading} or \textsc{The Nonperceptual Reading}, and similarly for \textsc{The Activity Reading}. And again, \emph{aisthēsis} might not admit of a univocal interpretation throughout. We thus face interpretative choices that affect our understanding of the overall structure of the aporetic reasoning. 

While the precise overall structure of the passage remains to be determined, at least two readings of the overall structure may be distinguished, even given the schematic representation of the aporetic reasoning.

First, there is a traditional reading the earliest extant version of which is given by Alexander of Aphrodisias (\emph{Quaestiones} 3.7). On the Alexandrian reading, (1) sets out the how-possible question to be investigated: How is it possible that we perceive that we see and hear? Two potential answers are offered: It is by \emph{opsis} that one perceives that one sees or by some other \emph{aesthēsis}. These alternatives seem to be exclusive (\citealt[401]{Osborne:1983le}; though Philoponus will deny this, \emph{In de anima}, 464,30–465,17). \textsc{The Duplication Argument} and \textsc{The Regress Arguments}, given in (2) and (3) respectively, are arguments against the second, higher-order alternative (see Alexander of Aphrodisias \emph{Quaestiones} 3.7, \citealt[433]{Hicks:1907uq}; for doubts, see \citealt{Kosman:2014ab} and \citealt{Osborne:1983le}). At which point we get the puzzle about the first reflexive alternative in (4), \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} familiar from the \emph{Charmides}. (5) and (6) make suggestions about how this \emph{aporia} might be mitigated, but it is not obvious that the \emph{aporia} is fully resolved by either suggestion or even if both suggestions are compatible.

What is distinctive about the Aleandrian reading is that \textsc{The Duplication Argument} and \textsc{The Regress Argument} are meant to be arguments against the higher-order alternative. There is room for disagreement about how the two suggestions resolve the \emph{aporia}. One might take \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} and \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} to be two suggestions about how to resolve the \emph{aporia} of \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}. According to Alexander (\emph{Quaestiones} 3.7 92,21–5), however, these are not suggestions about how to resolve a single difficulty, rather they are suggestions about how to resolve different difficulties. Specifically, \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} is meant to address the difficulty of some one activity being the apprehension of color and the apprehension of itself (discussed further in section~\ref{sub:the_argument_from_darkness}), whereas \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} is meant to address the \emph{aporia} of \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}.

Whereas Alexandrian reading understands (2)–(3) as arguments against the higher-order alternative, this understanding may be resisted. Thus the conclusion of \textsc{The Duplication Argument} in (2) is disjunctive, the disjuncts bearing on both alternatives (\citealt[401]{Osborne:1983le} and \citealt[765 n33]{caston02}). So against the Alexandrian reading, \textsc{The Duplication Argument} is less an argument against perceiving by \emph{opsis} by some other \emph{aesthēsis}, than a drawing out a consequence for both alternatives, that perceptual self-consciousness is transitive or transparent. Similarly for \textsc{The Regress Argument} of (3). On the Alexandrian reading, \textsc{The Regress Argument} is an argument against perceiving by \emph{opsis} by some other \emph{aisthēsis}. It is true that Aristotle rejects a series of higher-order perceptions that goes on to infinity, but he explicitly allows that the regress may be stopped if some higher-order perception at a finite level were reflexive. The point seems to be that if reflexivity at some finite level is what is required for a regress stopper, then the latter alternative has no obvious advantage over the former. Again, \textsc{The Regress Argument} is less an argument against the latter alternative, then a drawing out of a consequence for both, that each, at least properly developed, are committed to reflexivity.

\subsection{The How-Possible Question} % (fold)
\label{sub:the_how_possible_question}

\begin{quote}
	\textsc{The How-Possible Question}: Since we perceive that we see and hear, it must either be by (a) \emph{opsis} that one perceives that one sees or (b) some other [\emph{aisthēsis}]. (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b12–3)
\end{quote}

We perceive that we see and hear. Aristotle seems to accept this claim. About this claim, Aristotle poses a how-possible question: How is it possible that we perceive that we see and hear? Two alternative answers are offered, it is either by \emph{opsis} or some other \emph{aisthēsis} that we perceive that we see. How-possible questions are not posed out of the blue. If they are apt, then what occasions the question is a doubt or unclarity about that very possibility. It is the \emph{aporia} about reflexive intentional powers developed in the \emph{Charmides} that occasions and renders apt Aristotle's question. Indeed, a version of that puzzle is explicitly stated in step (4) of our schematic representation.

About the claim that gives rise to the how-possible question—the we perceive that we see and hear—there are questions about its scope, about how to understand perceiving, as well as whether the claim extends beyond seeing and hearing to each of the senses.

Begin with the scope of the claim. We perceive that we see and hear. Always? Or is it only some seeings and hearings that we perceive? As \citet[]{McCabe:2007jb} observes, the present tense of the verb does not settle the matter. If like Leibniz one distinguishes perception and apperception (\emph{Principes de la Nature et de la Grâce} §4, \emph{La Monadologie} §14), then one would reject that we always perceive that we see and hear:
\begin{quote}
	Thus it is well to make the distinction between \emph{perception}, which is the inner state of the Monad representing outer things, and \emph{apperception}, which is \emph{consciousness} or the reflective knowledge of this inner state, and which is not given to all souls nor to the same soul at all times. (\emph{Principes de la Nature et de la Grâce} §4, \citealt[411]{Latta:1898aa})
\end{quote}
So we may perceive that we see and hear, but only sometimes. By contrast, Sartre, standing at the culmination of a tradition that includes Augustine, Peter John Olivi, and Descartes, doubts the very coherence of the notion of an unconscious perception and so would maintain, instead, that we always perceive that we see and hear. According to Sartre, in order to perceive something one must perceive that one perceives: 
\begin{quote}
	It is a necessary condition: if my consciousness were not conscious of being conscious of a table, it would thereby be conscious of the table without being conscious that it was so or, alternatively, it would be consciousness that did not know itself, an unconscious consciousness—which is absurd. (Sartre, \emph{L'Être et le Néant} 18; Richmond, \citeyear{Sartre:2018aa}, 10)
\end{quote}
These alternative understandings bear on the modal status of the claim. If we always perceive that we see and hear, this can encourage understanding the claim as necessary as Sartre and other Cartesians do. That we only sometimes perceive that we see and hear, by contrast, can encourage understanding the claim as a contingent empirical claim (\citealt[401]{Osborne:1983le}). (Notice that the necessity that attaches to to the statement of (1) does not settle the matter as it takes wide scope: Necessarily [if we perceive that we see and hear, then it is either by \emph{opsis} that one perceives that one sees or by some other \emph{aisthēsis}]. This is importantly not the claim that that we necessarily perceive that we see and hear.) However, given the puzzle about reflexive powers as elaborated in \emph{Charmides}, the distinction matters little. If perceptual self-consciousness is reflexive, then that it always happens may be puzzling, but it remains so even if it only sometimes happens.

What does perceiving mean in Aristotle's claim that we perceive that we see and hear? First about the verb ``to perceive that'' (\emph{aisthanometha hoti} 425b12), it may be read more narrowly as strictly perceptual or more broadly as awareness more generally in a way that parallels \textsc{The Perceptual} and \textsc{The Nonperceptual Readings} of \emph{aisthēsis}. (Notice that this latter, broader reading is consistent with sight being the source of this awareness. There is after all a difference between seeing the seeing of a colored body and being aware of the seeing of that colored body, and sight might remain the source of this latter awareness.) \citet[265–6]{Shields:2016ix} rejects the perceptual understanding and suggests that the unusual construction (\emph{aisthanometha hoti}) forces the broader understanding of the relevant awareness citing \emph{Ethica Nichomachea} 1142\-a\-28 where proper (\emph{idion}) object perception (seeing color, hearing sound) is contrasted with perceiving that the triangle is the simplest figure (perhaps in the series of closed plane figures). (A potential worry is that Aristotle does not invariably use the \emph{hoti}-clause, e.g., \emph{hē tes opseos aisthēsis} 425b15–6. Another potential worry is that \emph{Ethica Nichomachea} 1142\-a\-28 might alternatively be read as contrasting proper object perception with common object perception.) 

Perhaps this difference in sense need not be resolved, at least initially. That is, perhaps, the reading of perceiving may be left open, to eventually be resolved by the proper resolution of the \emph{aporia}. On either reading, we can be aware, in some sense, that we see and hear.

\textsc{The How-Possible Question} involves a distinction: whether it is by \emph{opsis} that one perceives that one sees or by some some other [\emph{aisthēsis}]. Our understanding of that distinction can seem to depend upon our understanding of the occurence of \emph{opsis} which serves as the central contrast. Should \emph{opsis} be understood as the power of sight as \textsc{The Power Reading} recommends? Or should \emph{opsis} be understod as the proper exercise of that power, seeing, as \textsc{The Activity Reading} recommends? 

It might be thought that here there is less of a substantial difference between these readings than elsewhere. On \textsc{The Power Reading} we get a contrast between perceiving that we see being the exercise of the power of sight and being the exercise of some other perceptual power. Focus now on \textsc{The Activity Reading} where \emph{opsis} designates the proper exercise of the power of sight, seeing. If \emph{opsis} is seeing, then some other \emph{aisthēsis} is not seeing, but plausibly the proper exercise of some other perceptual power. After all, activity is prior in account to the power that produced it (\emph{De anima} 2.4 415a 16–22, for discussion of the Platonic background see \citealt[chapter 2]{Moss:2017aa}) So it would seem that, here at least, \textsc{The Power Reading} and \textsc{The Activity Reading} are in happy agreement: Perceiving that we see may either be the exercise of sight or some other perceptual power.

Unfortunately, this line of thought involves a substantive assumption that Aristotle, arguably at least, disavows. Specifically it assumes that a power has a single kind of exercise, in the unlovely vocabulary of modern metaphysics, that powers are single-track as opposed to multi-track. However, in \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} Aristotle maintains that to perceive by \emph{opsis} is no single thing (see section~\ref{sub:the_argument_from_darkness}). So it is fallacious to infer if \emph{opsis} is seeing and some other \emph{aisthēsis} is not seeing, that the latter must be the exercise of a perceptual power distinct from sight.

The challenge for \textsc{The Activity Reading} in the specific context of \textsc{The How Possible Question} is to explain how the reflexive alternative is not preemptively excluded. The opening \emph{aporia} of \emph{De anima} 3.2 is an elaboration of the puzzlement first introduced in the \emph{Charmides}. Recall, that puzzlement concerned how, if at all, psychic powers may be at once intentional and reflexive. So that aspect of the puzzlement should be preserved, in a way that it could not if the reflexive alternative was precluded at the very start. Too see how it might be, if \emph{opsis} is seeing as \textsc{The Activity Reading} recommends, then to perceive by \emph{opsis} that one sees would be to see one's seeing. But if these are distinct if co-occurring acts of sight, that can seem like the higher-order and not the reflexive alternative.

Aristotle assumes that if we perceive that we see, then it is either by \emph{opsis} or some other \emph{aisthēsis}. Taking at face value, it would seem that perceiving that we see is the exercise of a perceptual power. This is a substantive assumption that may be denied. Indeed, Philoponus (\emph{In de anima}, 464,30–465,17) denies it, attributing perceiving that we see and hear to the attentive part (\emph{tou prosektikou merous}) of reason (\emph{logos}) as opposed to any perceptual power (for discussion see \citealt{Bernard:1987hc}).

Is the object of awareness restricted to seeing and hearing, or is the claim applicable to the activities of all five senses? Do we perceive that we feel, taste, and smell as well as see and hear? That we plausibly at least sometimes do speaks in favor of generality (Philoponus, \emph{In de anima} 464, 18–20, Ibn Rushd, \emph{Commentarium Magnum in Aristotelis De Anima Libros}, 337). \citet[779 n59]{caston02}, however, demurs: ``Aristotle could not plausibly say that we `taste that we taste' or `smell that we smell' (i.e. smell that we are smelling)''. However, in \emph{Ethica Nicomachea} 9.9 1170a29-b21, Aristotle claims that just as one who sees can perceive that they see, one who walks can perceive that they walk. But if in walking we can be aware that we are walking, why in the cases of feeling, tasting, and smelling is it implausible that we can be aware of feeling, tasting, and smelling? If we can ``walk that we walk'', then why can we not ``taste that we taste'' or ``smell that we smell''?

\subsection{The Duplication Argument} % (fold)
\label{sub:the_duplication_argument}

\begin{quote}
	\textsc{The Duplication Argument}: But the same [\emph{aisthēsis}] will be of \emph{opsis} and the underlying color. So either (a) there will be two [\emph{aisthēseis}] of the same, or (b) the same [\emph{aisthēsis}] will be of itself. (\emph{De anima} 3.2 4225b13–15)
\end{quote}

According to \citet[435]{Hicks:1907uq}, Aristotle ``assumes that the sense which perceives sensation must perceive the object of sensation'', but worries whether this is self-evident. The assumption is a Platonic inheritance. For that the same will be of \emph{opsis} and the underlying color is an interim conclusion in the aporetic reasoning of the \emph{Charmides}. In applying Critias' account of \emph{sōphrosunē} to vision, Socrates posits a hypothetical form of vision that sees no color, but only itself and other visions and their lack (\emph{Charmides} 167c8–d2). Ordinary seeings are of color. The seeing of these seeings, however, do not involve seeing what they see. There is a failure of transitivity or transparency.  While I argued that the reflexive component of Critias' account was the primary target, the failure of transitivity or transparency in the case of perceptual self-consciousness may be no less troubling (indeed, some commentators, such as \citealt[202–3]{Sorabji:2006aa} and \citealt{Tsouna:2022aa}, see intransitivity or nontransparency, and not reflexivity, as the real difficulty being addressed in the \emph{Charmides}.)

Recall, in the discussion of the \textsc{Puzzling Disanalogies}, Socrates asks Critias to consider a vision of itself and other visions and their lack but is not of things that we see in the ordinary way (\emph{Charmides} 167c8–d2; see chapter~\ref{sub:perception}). The hypothetical vision is intransitive or nontransparent. It may take other visions as its objects but it does not, in turn, take the objects of these other visions as its own object. The vision of itself that takes the other sorts of vision as an object does not see through them to colorful scenes that they disclose.

The thought behind transitivity or transparency is straightforward and cogent, at least in the first-personal perceptual case (the complexities of the epistemic case were discussed in chapter 1.2 and 1.3.2). A third-personal, if not indeed a second-personal, case would be Socrates seeing Charmides' look that passes all description (\emph{Charmides} 155c7–d1). Socrates can, of course, see Charmides' look without seeing what Charmides sees. Transitivity or transparency holds, if at all, in the first-personal cases. Thus, in order to be aware of seeing a white thing (perhaps the son of Diares, \emph{De anima} 2.6 418a20–1) the perceiver must be aware of the white thing that they see. If the perceiver were not aware of the object of sight, how could they be aware that it was a seeing of a white thing as opposed to seeing of a red thing? Aquinas puts the general point well: ``For if it were not to have cognition of color, it could not have cognition of what it is to see; for to see is nothing other than to sense color'' (Aquinas, \emph{Sentencia libri De anima} 43, \citealt[303]{Pasnau:1999pd}; here Aquinas, wittingly or no, is echoing Alexander of Aphrodisias, \emph{Quaestiones} 3.7 92.9–10, \citealt[54]{Sharples:1994aa}). The identity of the perceptual activity is given, at least in part, by its object. So, in order for the awareness to discriminate the perceptual activity that is its object it must afford awareness of that activity's object.

\citet[122]{Hamlyn:1961ys} protests that ``one can clearly be aware that one is seeing without being aware of what one is seeing.'' There is at least an understanding of this that comes out true consistent with the truth of transitivity or transparency. But first let us reflect upon why this strikes Hamlyn as an objection. The thought would be that there is something that the perceiver sees, even if, in seeing it, the perceiver, in being aware of their so seeing, remains unaware what precisely they are seeing. So described that can seem like a failure of of transitivity or transparency. But the case is underdescribed for Hamlyn's purposes. Hamlyn's description is apt as well for a recognitional failure. Recognition is a mode of awareness. But crucially it is a mode of awareness that involves the application of a concept to what is present in our perceptual awareness. Thus a perceiver may see a white thing in the distance and fail to recognize that that white thing is the son of Diares (\emph{De anima} 2.6 418a20–1). But such a recognitional failure is consistent with transitivity or transparency in the first-personal perceptual case. The perceiver sees a white thing that they fail to recognize as the son of Diares. In being aware from within that they are seeing a white thing, that awareness is in part of the white thing that they see. This would be a case of being ``aware that one is seeing without being aware of what one is seeing''), but consistent with the truth of transitivity or transparency.

Just as in the \emph{Charmides}, transitivity or transparency is a positive feature to be preserved. And so it should be preserved by either of the alternatives on offer. That the same \emph{aisthēsis} will be of \emph{opsis} and the underlying color is explicitly said to hold of both the higher-order and reflexive alternatives (\citealt[401]{Osborne:1983le} and \citealt[765 n33]{caston02}). Recall that we perceive that we see either by \emph{opsis} or some other \emph{aisthēsis} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b12–3). So if by some other \emph{aisthēsis}, there will be two \emph{aisthēseis} of the same—that is, the seeing of the white thing and exercise of a sensory power distinct from sight that affords awareness of that seeing will both take the white thing as among their objects. On the previous alternative, where we perceive by \emph{opsis} that we see, the same \emph{aisthēsis} will be of itself—that is, the same seeing will be of the white thing and itself. So on the alternative where we perceive that we see by some other \emph{aisthēsis}, transparency is secured by a higher-order perception that takes seeing as its object and yet may peer through it to the colored object seen. And on the alternative where we perceive by \emph{opsis} that we see, transparency is secured by the seeing of the white thing reflexively applying to itself and so affording awareness from within of that very seeing. 

\textsc{The Duplication Argument} is sometimes presented as an argument against the higher-order alternative, where we perceive by some other \emph{aisthēsis} that we see (e.g., Alexander of Aphrodisias, \emph{Quaestiones} 3.7, \citealt[435]{Hicks:1907uq}, \citealt[121]{Hamlyn:1961ys}, and \citealt[275]{Ross:1961uq}). This is \emph{prima facie} odd since no explicit criticism is made in our passage (\emph{De anima} 3.2 4225b13–15). What implicit criticism might there be?

Philoponus makes a suggestion:
\begin{quote}
	He says it is not possible that one sense should see [colours] and another perceive the activity of sight, since [then] there would be no proper sense-objects, but all sense objects would be common. (Philoponus, \emph{In De anima} 463 7–9; \citealt[38]{Charlton:2013aa})
\end{quote}
Aristotle distinguishes incidental (\emph{kata sumbebēkota}) from proper (\emph{idion}) and common (\emph{koina}) sensibles. While the former are perceptible (\emph{De anima} 2.6 418a20–23, see also \emph{De anima} 3.1 425a23–27), only the proper and common objects of perception are perceptible in themselves (\emph{kath’ hauta}, \emph{De anima} 2.6 418a8–9). Proper and common sensibles are themselves distinguished. While proper objects are perceived by one sense alone, common objects may be perceived by more than one sense (\emph{De anima} 2.6 418a11–4, 418a18–21). Color is the proper object of sight. Since proper objects are perceived by one sense alone, and color is the proper object of sight, then it cannot be perceived by any other sense. According to \textsc{The Duplication Argument}, on the higher-order alternative, an episode of seeing color is perceived by some sense other than sight, and that sense not only perceives the seeing but also what is seen, the color. But then color would be perceived by more than one sense, making color not a proper but a common object. \citet[44]{Kosman:2014ab} thus echoes Philoponus when he claims that the positing a sense other than sight that perceives seeing and what is seen ``does violence to our notion of the proper object of sense'' (see also \citealt[121]{Hamlyn:1961ys}, and \citealt[275]{Ross:1961uq}).

The argument seems straightforward and cogent, at least at first blush. But is it genuinely attributable to Aristotle? \citet[402]{Osborne:1983le} has her doubts:
\begin{quote}
	However nothing is said about `proper' objects, and in the previous chapter we had just been considering objects perceived by more than one sense; there is nothing in the text [\emph{De anima} 3.2] to suggest that Aristotle finds this alternative more uncomfortable than the idea of a sense {\sbl αὐτή αὺτῆσ}. \citep[402]{Osborne:1983le}
\end{quote}
All of Osborne's observations are correct. However, for some, a residual worry may remain. Why not press an objection that Aristotle has the resources to make in light of \emph{De anima} 2.6? Dialectical context matters here. Suppose, as I have suggested, that, just like in the \emph{Charmides}, transparency or transitivity is a positive feature to be preserved by any adequate alternative. That, as a consequence, on the higher-order alternative, transparency or transitivity must hold does not require assurance that it does hold on the higher-order alternative in a manner that is consistent with the doctrines of \emph{De anima} 2.6.

\subsection{The Regress Argument} % (fold)
\label{sub:the_regress_argument}

\begin{quote}
	\textsc{The Regress Argument}: Further, if the \emph{aisthēsis} which is of \emph{opsis} is different, either (a) this will go on to infinity or (b) some \emph{aisthēsis} will be of itself. So one should grant this of the first. (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b15-7)
\end{quote}

\textsc{The Duplication Argument} establishes that transparency can be secured either by the higher-order alternative where we perceive by some other \emph{aisthēsis} that we see or by the reflexive alternative were where we perceive by \emph{opsis} that we see. \textsc{The Regress Argument} in turn establishes that the higher-order alternative, properly developed to avoid a problematic infinite regress, must itself have a reflexive component. A defeasible consideration in favor of the reflexive alternative is given (``So one should grant this of the first''). This in turn sets up the \emph{aporia} about reflexivity in \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} (\emph{De Anima} 3.2 425b17–20).

\textsc{The Regress Argument} is not without precedent in the Aristotelian corpus. Similar arguments about infinite series can be found in \emph{Analytica posteriora} 1.3 72b8–14, \emph{Physica} 8.5 256a4–b2, \emph{Metaphysica} B.2 994a1–19, and \emph{Ethica Nichomachea} 1.2 1094a18–21.

An obstacle to understanding \textsc{The Regress Argument} is that Aristotle does not explicitly state the principle that generates the infinite series. What might that principle be? This is important, since without some such principle, the regress may stop after the very first step. On the higher-order alternative, in order to perceive that one sees, there may be a perceiving distinct from the seeing that takes the seeing as its object, but if stops there, there is no infinite regress (see Kosman's \citeyear[280–1]{Kosman:2005ff} criticism of Johansen's \citeyear{Johansen:2005hz} reconstruction of \textsc{The Regress Argument} and \citealt[206]{Sorabji:2006aa}). Indeed, the other arguments in the Aristotelian corpus involving an infinite series (\emph{Analytica posteriora} 1.3 72b8–14, \emph{Physica} 8.5 256a4–b2, \emph{Metaphysica} B.2 994a1–19, and \emph{Ethica Nichomachea} 1.2 1094a18–21), state such a principle where that principle typically involves a relation of priority between later and earlier members of the series.

What might the relevant principle be in \textsc{The Regress Argument} of \emph{De anima} 3.2 425b15-7? Themistius makes a suggestion. According to Themistius: 
\begin{quote}
	It is not possible that sight sees without being aware of what is seen ({\sbl οὐ γὰρ οἷόν τε ἀποφήνασϑαι περὶ τῆς ὄψεως ὅτι ὁρᾷ μὴ γινώσχουσοαν τὸ ὁρώμενον} \emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima paraphrasis} 83,15–6, \citealt{Heinze:1899aa}).
\end{quote}
\citet[435]{Hicks:1907uq}, instead, understands this claim to justify transitivity or transparency in the higher-order case. But it manifestly does not say that in order for a sense other than \emph{opsis} to be aware of a seeing it must be aware of the object of that seeing (for mockery, see \citealt[44–5]{Kosman:2014ab}). Hicks misparses Themistius' paraphrase. The principle does not purport to provide an explanation for transitivity or transparency, but rather the very next claim, Themistius' statement of \textsc{The Regress Argument}, immediately follows on from it:
\begin{quote}
	And if sight needs another [sense] to discern [sight], why does the sense that [so] discerns not also need another sense, and that one another, in a progression to infinity? (Themistius \emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima paraphrasis} 83,16–8, \citealt{Heinze:1899aa}, \citealt[105]{Todd:1996aa})
\end{quote}

In order for sight to see what it does the perceiver must be aware that they are so seeing. Being aware of so seeing is a matter of that seeing being the object of \emph{aisthēsis}. But the \emph{aisthēsis} which is of \emph{opsis} is different (\emph{De anima} 425b15). So on \textsc{The Activity Reading}, there is a perception of a seeing that is not the exercise of sight. Now from Themistius' principle, in order for that perception to be of so seeing, the perceiver must be aware of so perceiving. But if that is a matter of a further \emph{aisthēsis} that is the exercise of a further sense, then seeing and being aware of so seeing would involve infinitely many acts that are the exercise of infinitely many powers. What would stop the regress is if some some \emph{aisthēsis} were of itself (\emph{De Anima} 425b 16–7, compare how reflexivity, in this case the existence of a self-mover, stops a similar regress in \emph{Physica} 8.5 256a4–b2). But if some \emph{aisthēsis} will be of itself, why could not the initial seeing be of itself? So in seeing what it does, one must be aware of so seeing. And one is aware of so seeing not by an \emph{aisthēsis} of \emph{opsis} that is different. It is the very same seeing that is of what it sees and reflexively applies to itself in affording the perceiver awareness of so seeing. 

Is Themistius' principle true or at least plausible? This is relevant to what I earlier described as the scope of the claim that we perceive that we see and hear (section~\ref{sub:the_how_possible_question}) Do we always? Or is it only some seeings and hearings that we perceive? Notice that Themistius' principle speaks in favor of always. If like Leibniz one distinguishes perception and apperception (\emph{Principes de la Nature et de la Grâce} §4, \emph{La Monadologie} §14), then one would reject that we always perceive that we see and hear. Among recent commentators, \citet{caston02} sides with Themistius and \citet{Johansen:2005hz} with Leibniz. Indeed, it is Johansen's \citeyearpar{Johansen:2005hz} Leibnizian commitment to the distinction between perception and apperception that makes his reconstruction of \textsc{The Regress Argument} subject to Kosman's \citeyearpar{Kosman:2005ff} criticism. It is no surprise that a variant \textsc{The Regress Argument} recurs millennia hence in chapter one of \emph{L'Être et le Néant} by the avowed Cartesian Sartre. (Contemporary defenders of the higher-order theory of consciousness typically avoid the regress by denying that the higher-order state that confers consciousness on a first-order state are themselves conscious, see for example \citealt{Rosenthal:1986jt}.)

\textsc{The Regress Argument} establishes that the higher-order alternative—where we perceive by some other \emph{aisthēsis} that we see—is like the reflexive alternative—where we perceive by \emph{opsis} that we see—in involving a reflexive component. Both are committed to reflexivity, at least properly developed to avoid an infinite regress. So just as \textsc{The Duplication Argument} establishes that transitivity or transparency holds of both the higher-order and reflexive alternatives, \textsc{The Regress Argument} establishes that reflexivity holds of both as well. This is important since the only bit of the reasoning that is explicitly described as aporetic, indeed the immediately subsequent passage, \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b17–20), targets reflexivity.

\subsection{The Argument from Relatives} % (fold)
\label{sub:the_argument_from_relatives}

\begin{quote}
	\textsc{The Argument from Relatives}: But there is a puzzle. For if to perceive by \emph{opsis} is to see, and if it is color or what has color that is seen, then if one is to see that which sees (\emph{to horōn}), that which sees (\emph{to horōn}) must have color in the primary way. (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b17–20)
\end{quote}

The label for this passage, \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}, is contentious but ultimately justified given the origin of this line of thought in the \emph{Charmides} (168b2–169c2). That argument cast doubt on whether intentional psychic powers could be reflexive, where reflexive powers apply to themselves or or at least their exercise. Perception is a psychic power characteristic of animals. Moreover, perception is of something. Perception is thus an intentional power since its exercise takes an object. In seeing, there is something seen. In hearing, there is something heard. In the \emph{Charmides}, \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} proceeds from a general premise that in order for a power to be of something that thing must have a certain nature or being (\emph{ousia}). So if an intentional power applies either to itself or to its exercise, these must themselves have the relevant nature or being (\emph{Charmides} 168d1–5). What is seen by sight is colored (\emph{Charmides} 168d9–e2). So if in seeing the colored scene one sees as well the seeing of it, this perceptual activity, seeing, must itself be colored.

Compare now our passage. \textsc{The Regress Argument} that precedes it raises the possibility that some \emph{aisthēseis} will be of themselves. \emph{Aisthēsis} is the exercise of an intentional psychic power. So the possibility raised is that some \emph{aisthēseis} are reflexive. But this is what gives rise to the \emph{aporia}. This is the only element of \emph{De anima} 3.2 425b12–25 that is explicitly described as aporetic. (Perhaps this is the reason that Ps-Simplicius' linear commentary on this chapter begins with this passage.) So we are confronting a puzzle about whether intentional psychic powers might also be reflexive, just like in the \emph{Charmides} (168b2–169c2). Moreover, the puzzle that Aristotle describes turns on the claim that seeing is of color or colored things. But this is an instance of the generality that drove the puzzle about how to coherently combine intentionality and reflexivity in the \emph{Charmides}. In order for a power to be of something it must have a certain nature or being. In the case of sight, the relevant nature or being is color or being colored. Seeing is of color or colored things because only such things have the nature or being required for sight to be of them. Furthermore, the argument proceeds in the way it did in the \emph{Charmides}. If seeing is of color or colored things, then reflexive seeings must themselves be colored. But that is absurd.

Aristotle is implicitly committed to the general claim that in order for an intentional psychic power to be of something, it must have a certain nature or being (\emph{ousia}). For consider the senses other than sight. In the case of audition, the relevant nature or being is sound or being sonorous and not color or being colored. This is a different instance of the relevant generality. So in order for \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} to be fully general, applicable to every instance of reflexive perceptual intentionality, it must be reconstructed with reference to the general claim that is the fundamental premise of the \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} as developed in the \emph{Charmides}.

The parallel with the \emph{Charmides} is formal. Its abstract character obscures an important linguistic difference. Unlike, in the \emph{Charmides}, the thought is that to perceive by \emph{opsis} that we see involves seeing that which sees (\emph{to horōn}). Whether and to what extent Aristotle's puzzlement departs from the Socratic puzzlement described in the \emph{Charmides} depends, in part, in how \emph{to horōn} is understood.

While \emph{to horōn} is unproblematically translated as ``that which sees'', its interpretation remains contestable. There are four readings:
\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
	\item \textsc{The Perceiver Reading}: That which sees (\emph{to horōn}) is the agent of the perceptual activity, the perceiver, or more specifically, the seer.
	\item \textsc{The Power Reading}: That which sees (\emph{to horōn}) is the power, sight, by which the perceiver sees what they do.
	\item \textsc{The Activity Reading}: That which sees (\emph{to horōn}) is the activity of sight, the seeing
	\item \textsc{The Corporeal Reading}: That which sees (\emph{to horōn}) is the corporeal instrument, the sense organ, the eyes, by which the perceiver exercises their power of sight in seeing what they do.
\end{enumerate}

These readings are, of course, not unrelated. \textsc{The Perceiver Reading} is perhaps the most natural, flat-footed reading of \emph{to horōn} considered out of context. So understood, \emph{to horōn} is perhaps better translated as ``the seer'', being the animal that sees. ``That which sees'' would be awkward on \textsc{The Perceiver Reading} to say the least (compare describing one's mother, a housewife, as that which cleans). I doubt that it is in play in our passage (\emph{De Anima} 3.2 425b17–20). If ``the seer'' is a correct translation of \emph{to horōn} here, it could only function as a synecdoche, the whole standing for the part. On the other readings, the power, activity, or corporeal instrument count as \emph{to horōn} insofar as they are the power, activity, or corporeal instrument of the seer relevant to their seeing what they do. Thus on \textsc{The Corporeal Reading}, that which sees are the eyes, the corporeal instruments of sight, with which the seer sees what they do. And on \textsc{The Power Reading}, that which sees is the power, sight, that is enabling the perceiver to see what they do. \textsc{The Activity Reading} is perhaps the least natural at first blush. On that reading, \emph{to horōn} is the seeing of the seer, the exercise of the power of sight, the seeing of what they do. \textsc{The Activity Reading}, here, gains some support by being in line with what is at issue in \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} in the \emph{Charmides}.

\textsc{The Corporeal Reading} in turn gains some support by its role in \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b22-5). There it is claimed that \emph{to horōn} is in a sense colored since the sense organ (\emph{aisthētērion}) receives the form without the matter of the sense object. At least, that instance of \emph{to horōn}, if translated as the seer, is a synecdoche, the whole standing for the part, namely the relevant sense organ. But what support \textsc{The Corporeal Reading} gains is slight as this may be consistent with \emph{to horōn} functioning as a different if related synecdoche in \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}.

We shall postpone how best to understand \emph{to horōn} in \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} until we discuss \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} given the potential for occurrences of \emph{to horōn} in these passages to receive coordinate readings.

\subsection{The Argument from Darkness and Light} % (fold)
\label{sub:the_argument_from_darkness}

\begin{quote}
	\textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light}: But it is clear that to perceive by \emph{opsis} is no single thing; for even when we do not see, it is by \emph{opsis} that we discriminate dark and light, though not in the same way. (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b20–2)
\end{quote}

This is the first of two suggestions about how the \emph{aporia} raised by \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} might be resolved. As we shall see, these two suggestions pull in different directions and a doubt may be raised about their coherence with one another.

% To perceive by \emph{opsis} is no single thing. Notice what Aristotle does not say. He does not say that perceiving by sight is spoken of in many ways.

To perceive by \emph{opsis} is no single thing. To see is to perceive by \emph{opsis}—to that extent the supposition that generates the \emph{aporia} is correct. But not every case of perceiving by \emph{opsis} is a seeing. That is the insight that is meant to at least partially resolve the \emph{aporia}.

Allow me to make an observation and a clarification.

The observation is that the power of sight has multiple manifestations. Seeing is the exercise of sight. But so is the perceiving by \emph{opsis} that is not a seeing. In the unlovely vocabulary of modern metaphysics, sight is a multi-track power (on the application of this notion to Aristotle, see \citealt[3.1.2]{Marmodoro:2014br}). Aristotle seems anyway committed to this. Consider the perception of the common sensibles. The visual perception of the common sensibles is distinct from the seeing of colors. Whereas seeing presents the colors, the perception of shape, say, presents shape and not color, even if these experiences co-occur.

The clarification concerns seeing. The possibility that is meant to at least partially resolve the \emph{aporia} is the possibility of perceiving by \emph{opsis} that is not seeing. But that is only as clear as our understanding of seeing that perceiving by \emph{opsis} is denied to be. So what is the relevant notion of seeing? Seeing is perceiving color in light. The \emph{aporia} turned on the fact that seeing is of color or of colored things. And color is visible in light (\emph{De anima} 2.7 418a26–b3). Color is the power to move the actually transparent, and light, understood as a state of illumination, is the actually transparent.

% If seeing is not just perceiving by \emph{opsis}, then how are we to understand what seeing is? 

The proposed resolution of the \emph{aporia} should now be clear, at least in outline (\emph{pace} \citealt[122]{Hamlyn:2002ys}). If we perceive by \emph{opsis} that which sees, and perceiving by \emph{opsis} is seeing, and seeing is of color or colored things, then that which sees must itself be colored. But, if we can perceive by \emph{opsis} in a way that is not seeing, and if, more specifically, we can perceive by \emph{opsis} that which sees without seeing it, then no commitment to it being colored is incurred.

That seeing is of color or colored things is, recall, an instance of a more general claim: That an intentional psychic power is of something that has a certain nature or being. If perceiving by \emph{opsis} is not seeing, then it is not required that the seeing perceived by \emph{opsis} must itself be colored. Nonetheless, the general claim may yet be true. And if we perceive by \emph{opsis} that we see, then seeing must have a certain nature or being in order for this to be so.

That not every exercise of \emph{opsis} need be a seeing suffices to block \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}. Moreover, an example suffices to establish this. The example need not be reflexive. It may be intentional. But so long as it is not a seeing requiring its objects to be colored, the aporetic reasoning is blocked.

The difficulty posed by \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} is less understanding the general outline of the proposed resolution, than understanding the example that is meant to establish that there is a perceiving by \emph{opsis} that is not seeing—that we discriminate dark and light, though not in the same way.

The most straightforward example establishing this would be a case where one perceives by \emph{opsis} in the absence of seeing. But, importantly, that is not required. Perceiving by \emph{opsis} may be distinct from seeing and yet occur along with it. So there are two ways to understand the case. It is either a case where:
\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
	\item Perceiving by \emph{opsis} occurs in the absence of seeing, or
	\item Perceiving by \emph{opsis} is distinct from seeing and yet may occur along with it
\end{enumerate}
And there are at least two ways to understand perceiving by \emph{opsis} in the absence of seeing. It is either a case where:
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
	\item Under conditions of excessive darkness or light we perceive by \emph{opsis} that we do not see the colors in the scene before us, or
	\item Under conditions of low light, only relative brightness, and not chromatic hue, is perceived
\end{enumerate}
The alternative where perceiving by \emph{opsis} is not seeing color but may occur along with it is a case where:
\begin{enumerate}[(c)]
	\item A sensible, distinct from the colors, is perceived by \emph{opsis}.
\end{enumerate}
A hybrid interpretation combining elements of (a) and (c) is also possible:
\begin{enumerate}[(d)]
	\item Under conditions of excessive darkness, we perceive by \emph{opsis} that we do no see color, and under normal illumination, we perceive by \emph{opsis} light, a sensible distinct from color.
\end{enumerate}
Let us consider these alternatives in turn.


(a) \emph{Under conditions of excessive darkness or light we perceive by \emph{opsis} that we do not see the colors in the scene before us.} A transparent body, such as air or water, is actually transparent due to the presence of a fiery substance. When the fiery substance is absent, darkness supervenes. If color is the power to move what is actually transparent, then in complete darkness, there is nothing for the chromatic powers to move and are thus inactive. The perceiver is thus incapable of seeing the colors of the scene before them when shrouded in darkness. And yet, darkness is something that we visually experience. We thus perceive the darkness by \emph{opsis}. Thus if seeing is of color or of colored things, then the perceiver's visual experience of darkness is not a seeing—it is at least not the seeing of the inactive colors of the scene before them. (Alexander \emph{Quaestiones} 3.7, Aquinas \emph{Sentencia libri De anima}, 73–97; see also \citealt[402]{Osborne:1983le}.)

Alexander of Aphrodisias develops this thought in an interesting way. Alexander understands \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} as explaining how the characteristic exercise of sight, seeing, may at once be intentional—it is a seeing of a colored body—and afford reflexive awareness of so seeing—it affords awareness from within of seeing that body (\emph{Quaestiones} 3.7 92,21–5). If color is the proper object of sight, and color is seen in the light, then \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} is meant to establish that a sense can sense the privation of its object, since the colors of the scene before one are inactive when deprived of light. 

Connecting perceptual self-consciousness with the privation of the proper object of sight requires a further thought, however. The privation of light incapacitates the chromatic powers and so deprives the awake and attentive animal of sight in the sense of seeing. For seeing is of colored things and the colors are not acting upon their eyes. The implicit thought is that sight is sensitive to its own inactivity in not seeing the chromatic scene. Perceiving darkness by \emph{opsis} is a manifestation of sight's sensitivity to its own inactivity in not seeing colors in the surrounding darkness. Darkness perception essentially involves attributing to sight a reflexive sensitivity to its own inactivity. Once we understand that, we can understand how seeing may both be of a colored thing and of itself. If sight is reflexively sensitive to the privation of its activity, it is at the very least plausible that it is reflexively sensitive as well to its activity, and that perceiving by \emph{opsis} that we see is a manifestation of this. As Themistius observes: ``Thus we perceive that we are not seeing by the very same sense by which we also perceive that we are seeing, and this [sense] will be sight'' (\emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima paraphrasis} 83, 24–5; \citealt[105]{Todd:1996aa}). 

The argument may be strengthened in the following way. Suppose that sight's sensitivity to its own activity explains its sensitivity to its inactivity, so that it is a condition on the possibility of reflexive sensitivity to inactivity. Then in establishing a reflexive sensitivity to sight's inactivity, a reflexive sensitivity to sight's activity is thereby established as well, since the later is a condition on the possibility of the former. Whereas the conclusion of the previous argument merely makes plausible that there be reflexive sensitivity to sight's activity, knowledge of this reflexive sensitivity would be warranted on the basis of the present argument if sound.

There is a complication that raises a potential problem. The visual experience of darkness is not a seeing of the inactive colors of the scene before them. That much is true. But darkness is not achromatic. It is black. Contrast the visual experience of darkness with the auditory experience of silence. Whereas darkness has a visible quality, silence lacks an auditory quality. (What pitch is it? What timbre? See \citealt{Sorensen:2009aa} for further discussion of this contrast.). Or contrast being unable to see in the dark with being asleep or unconscious. When asleep or unconscious, the animal is not undergoing a chromatic experience of the surrounding darkness.

That darkness is black should be especially salient in a linguistic context where dark and light are the exemplars of black and white. But in perceiving by \emph{opsis} the darkness that obscures the multicolored scene before one, one perceives a black thing. But would not perceiving by \emph{opsis} a black thing, and so a colored thing, simply be a case of seeing something colored? In which case perceiving darkness would not be a case of perceiving by \emph{opisis} that is not seeing something colored. Or at least not obviously.

Perhaps the way out of this problem is to retain the focus on not seeing the colored scene before one in the darkness since, in the absence of light, the chromatic powers arrayed in that scene are inactive. In perceiving the darkness, one is aware of not seeing the colors of the scene before one, even if darkness is itself a black thing. And the awareness of not seeing the colors of the scene before one is a perceiving by \emph{opsis} that is not itself a seeing. Darkness may be black, but not seeing the colors of the scene before one is without color, and so an awareness of the latter would be a perceiving by \emph{opsis} that is not seeing, where seeing is of color or of something colored.

Perceiving darkness may be a case of perceiving by \emph{opsis} that is not seeing the colored scene before one, but what about perceiving light? In the case of total darkness, it was the absence of light that prevented the colors from acting upon what is actually transparent, and so prevented the perceiver from seeing the colored scene before them. But in the present case, light is not absent but present and perceived. So how is the perception of light an obstacle to seeing the colored scene before one? (Aquinas remains silent on this question.)

% (though as we shall see a doubt may be raised)

Later in the chapter (\emph{De Anima} 3.2 426b1–2), in the course of arguing that perception is a kind of ratio, Aristotle claims that an excess of brightness or darkness destroys sight (by destroying the ratio that enables sight). Perhaps, perceiving light by \emph{opsis} that is not seeing is meant to be an experience of excessive brightness that obscures the colored scene before one. (Compare Plato's description of the dazzling experience of brightness, \emph{lampros}, or brilliance, \emph{stilbos}, \emph{Timaeus} 68a1–6, see \citealt[72–7]{Kalderon:2022kl}.) In the darkness, a flashlight suddenly shown in a person's face can obscure the person weilding it. In such a case, one is blinded by the light.

If that is how we are to understand lightness perception, then one can understand the qualification, ``though not in the same way'', as follows. Perceiving excessive darkness by \emph{opsis} is not seeing the colored scene in the same way that perceiving excessive light by \emph{opsis} is. The obstacle to seeing is different in each case. They are different ways of destroying the ratio that constitutes the power to see color. 

Against this Ross writes:
\begin{quote}
	In light of 422a21–23 \ldots, one might be tempted to think that {\sbl άλλ´ούχ ώσαύτως} \ldots means `but we do not perceive darkness in the same way as light'. But that is not A.'s point here; his point is that the perception of light and darkness is different from ordinary sight, which is the perception of \emph{colour}. \citep[275]{Ross:1961uq}
\end{quote}
Perceiving by \emph{opsis} is no single thing. There is seeing color or colored things in contrast, say, to perceiving by \emph{opsis} darkness and lightness (and, importantly, to perceiving by \emph{opsis} that we see). Ross insists that {\sbl άλλ´ούχ ώσαύτως} is marking just this distinction. In contrasting lightness and darkness perception with ordinary sight, Ross is not denying that lightness and darkness perception are the exercise of the power of sight, rather he explains ordinary sight as the seeing of color. Ordinary sight is seeing color. And so lightness and darkness and perception and seeing color may all be manifestations of sight, it is just that seeing color is the characteristic activity of that power. Thus the qualification is emphasizing that \emph{opis} is a multi-track power.

On the present reading, sight is only partially incapacitated. Sight is incapacitated in that, under conditions of excessive darkness or brightness, one cannot see the colors arrayed in the scene before one. But sight remains in operation. One visually experiences the darkness. And the visual experience of brilliance is dazzling. So the perceiving that is not the seeing of color remains the exercise of the power of sight. Otherwise we would lose the contrast with the different ways that sight is incapacitated when asleep, or having fainted, or rendered unconscious by having the veins in one's neck compressed (\emph{De somno et vigilia} 455b). When asleep or unconscious one does no perceive by \emph{opsis} the excessive darkness or brightness that surrounds one, let alone see the colors of the ambient scene.

Can the distinction between seeing color and perceiving by \emph{opsis} be sustained on this reading? A doubt may be raised given the phenomenology of our experience of excessive brightness. Earlier we observed that darkness is chromatic—it is black. And arguably, so too for excessive brightness. Timaeus claims that brilliance causes all sorts of colors to appear (\emph{Timaeus} 68a5–6). But perceiving excessive brightness was mant to be a case of perceiving by \emph{opsis} that did not involve the visual awareness of color.

The problem admits of the same solution suggested in the case of perceiving darkness. Again, retain the focus on not seeing the colored scene before one in excessive brightness. In excessive brightness, it is less that the chromatic powers arrayed in the scene are inactive than their activity is masked (in Johnston's \citeyear{Johnston:1992ck} sense, see also \citealt{Bird:1998aa}) by excessive brilliance. In being dazzled by excessive brightness, one is aware of not seeing the colors of the scene before one, even if the dazzling brightness causes all kinds of colors to appear as Timaeus observes. And the awareness of not seeing the colors of the scene before one is a perceiving by \emph{opsis} that is not itself a seeing. Brightness may be multicolored as Timaeus contends, but not seeing the colors of the scene before one is without color, and so an awareness of the latter would be a perceiving by \emph{opsis} that is not seeing.

(b) \emph{Under conditions of low light, only relative brightness, and not chromatic hue, is perceived.} The perception of darkness and light is meant to be a case of perceiving by \emph{opsis} in the absence of seeing color. The first variant sought to establish this by excessive darkness and brightness obscuring the colored scene set before the perceiver, so that no element of that scene was in fact perceived. The second variant allows that elements of the scene be perceptually discriminated in the way that the initial variant precludes. So consider looking at a scene in low light. We can perceive the relative brightness of objects in a sufficiently dark room, even if we cannot distinguish their hue. (This suggestion is due to \citealt[436]{Hicks:1907uq}, no doubt due to his own experience of fading sight—he was blind by the time he wrote his \emph{De anima} translation and commentary.) 

\emph{La nuit, tous les chats sont gris}. ``At night, all cats are gray.'' Even the neighbor's calico cat appears gray at night. On this variant, we perceive dark and light but not the chromatic hues. We thus have an example of perceiving relative brightness by \emph{opsis} without seeing color, where seeing color is understood as, specifically, seeing chromatic hues. That darkness is black is no objection. Moreover, the qualification ``though not in the same way'' may be read, not as contrasting the perception of darkness from the perception of light, but by contrasting each with seeing color as \citet[275]{Ross:1961uq} recommends.

In this way is it an advance over the initial variant. And yet difficulties remain. The present reading assimilaties discriminating darkness and light to discriminating darkness from light. But that is not what the text says. Moreover, the idea that black, white, and gray are achromatic colors that contrast with the chromatic hues such as red and green seems alien to a color scheme that takes the colors to be mixtures of black and white understood as light and dark.

(c) \emph{A sensible, distinct from the colors, is perceived by \emph{opsis}.} On this alternative, the example is not meant to be a case of perceiving by \emph{opsis} in the absence of seeing color. Rather, perceiving by \emph{opsis}, though distinct from seeing color, may occur along with it. The objects of perceiving by \emph{opsis} are darkness and light as opposed to the colors (Philoponus, \emph{In de anima} 463, 38–464,2; Ibn Rushd, \emph{Commentarium Magnum in Aristotelis De Anima Libros}, 240; \citealt[249–50]{Johansen:2005hz}, though he assumes, without necessity or comment, that the lightness and darkness perception occur in the absence of color perception, thus collapsing (c) into (a)). 

Begin with the perception of light. In a transparent medium illuminated by the presence of the fiery substance, the chromatic powers of the scene move what is actually transparent and so affect the organ of sight, the eyes, thus bringing about the seeing of that scene. This is a clear case of seeing a colored thing. In seeing the calico cat in daylight, say, I see its tripartite color. But I also visually experience the daylight. But perhaps I do not see the daylight despite seeing the colors seen through it. Light may be colored in a way (\emph{De anima} 2.7 418b11) but only in a sense that contrasts with the primary sense in which the cat is tricolored. That light is only in a sense colored is insufficient for the perception of light to count as seeing color. How, then, is light presented in the daylit scene, if not by being colored in the primary sense? Perhaps, it is by being moved by chromatic powers in a manner that affects sight that light is perceived by \emph{opsis}.

% After all, light could not be colored in the primary sense. In the primary sense, color is the power to move what is actually transparent. Light is the actual transparent. To be colored, light would have to have the power to move itself in a way that Aristotle deems impossible.

Consider then the perception of darkness. Recall that on the present reading perception by \emph{opsis} is distinct from seeing color, though it may occur along with it. Unlike the first reading, it need not be a case of perception by \emph{opsis} in the absence of seeing. It was that thought that prompted the idea that darkness was total in a manner incompatible with seeing. But having abandoned the first reading, there is no longer the pressure to understand darkness as total darkness. So consider the calico cat. It is sitting next to a stone wall, perfectly still, eyes closed, enjoying the sunlight. Since it is late afternoon, the cat casts an elongated shadow. Darkness does not so much as obscure this scene as it is a perceived element of it. The shadow is dark. But it is not totally dark. The rough texture of the stone wall may still be discerned despite the cat's shadow being cast upon it. Its shadow is not so much the total absence of light as the diminution of light relative to the overall ambient illumination. After all, shadows may persist despite a degree of light pollution. What is within the cat's cast shadow is illuminated by significantly less light than the overall scene. Shadows may obscure a scene—one may hide in the shadows—but only to a degree. Darker shadows may obscure the color of what lies within but not so their paler siblings. If light is revealed to sight by the colors seen through it, dark is revealed to sight by the relative resistance it offers to seeing color, a resistance which is frequently less than total.

The qualification ``though not in the same way'' here must be read, not as contrasting the perception of darkness from the perception of light, but by contrasting each with seeing color as \citet[275]{Ross:1961uq} recommends.

On this reading, the perception by \emph{opsis} of darkness and light is like, if not exactly like, the visual perception of the common sensibles (on the common, \emph{koina}, sensibles see \emph{De anima} 2.5 417a10–20, 3.3 428b 22–30, \emph{De sensu} 437a9, 552b4–10, \emph{De memoria} 450a9–12). There is no perceiving the shape of a thing without seeing the color that pervades its surface. But color and not shape is the proper (\emph{idion}) object of vision. Similarly, there is no perceiving the light that pervades a scene withut seeing the colored objects it illuminates. But color and not light is the proper object of vision. Light may be perceptible to one sense alone, and so not itself a common sensible, but it is not a proper sensible either.
 % is not perceptible in itself, and so is not a proper sensible. Light does not contain within itself the power of its own visibility. Light is perceptible only insofar as it enables the colors to be perceptible by moving it. Moreover, sight is for the sake of seeing colors, not perceiving light. Light, like the common sensibles, is only ever presented to sight insofar as color is seen, but unlike the common sensibles, it is sensed by no other sense. And sight is for the sake of neither.


This reading, however, is not without difficulty. A question may be raised about how it coheres with Aristotle's taxonomy of the objects of perception (\emph{De Anima} 2.6). Specifically he distinguishes proper (\emph{idion}) and common (\emph{koina}) objects and both are contrasted with incidental (\emph{kata sumbebēkota}) objects. Incidental (\emph{kata sumbebēkota}) sensibles are perceptible but not perceptible in themselves. The pale thing seen in the distance is the son of Diares (\emph{De anima} 2.6 418a20–23, see also \emph{De anima} 3.1 425a23–27). In that sense, the son of Diares is perceived. But while paleness may be a power to move what is actually transparent, being the son of Diares is not. That is why being the son of Diares, while perceptible, is not perceptible in itself—it does not contain within itself the power of its own visibility. By contrast, the proper and common sensibles are perceptible in themselves (\emph{kath' hauta}, \emph{De anima} 2.6 418a8–9). But this class divides. Either they are perceptible to one sense alone in which case they are proper (\emph{idion}) objects (\emph{De Anima} 2.6 418a11–4), or they are perceptible to more than one sense, in which case they are common (\emph{koina}) objects (\emph{De anima} 2.6 418a18–21).

Darkness and lightness are perceptible. Unlike incidental (\emph{kata sumbebēkota}) sensibles, they are perceptible in themselves. They are, more specifically visible. But the proper (\emph{idion}) object of vision is color (and perhaps that which has no name but is seen in the dark, \emph{De anima} 2.7 418a26–7, 419a2–7). But darkness and lightness are visibilia distinct from the colors (and that which has no name). So they are not the proper (\emph{idion}) objects of vision. However, darkness and lightness are perceptible to one sense alone, sight. So they are not common (\emph{koina}) objects. The puzzle is that the principles that generate the taxonomy seem to induce an exhaustive ordering in which darkness and lightness find no place. 

The puzzle might be resolved by denying that darkness and lightness are perceptible in themselves (\emph{kath' hauta}). If light is not perceptible in itself, then light does not contain within itself the power of its own visibility. Rather, light is perceptible only insofar as it enables the colors to be perceptible by moving it. Moreover, sight is for the sake of seeing colors, not perceiving light. Light, like the common sensibles, is only ever presented to sight insofar as color is seen, but unlike the common sensibles, it is sensed by no other sense. And sight is for the sake of neither.

Does the final consideration really support that darkness and lightness are not perceptible in themselves (\emph{kath' hauta})? Sight may be for the sake of seeing color and not light, but neither is it for the sake of seeing shape. So common sensibles would also count as incidental by this criterion (compare \emph{De anima} 3.1 xx), but common sensibles are perceptible in themselves (\emph{De anima}, 2.6 xx). So the perception of something visible that sight is not for the sake of is not, by itself, reason to think that that the visible thing does not contain within itself the power of its own visibility.

In denying that darkness and lightness are perceptible in themselves (\emph{kath' hauta}), they are assimilated to incidental (\emph{kata sumbebēkota}) sensibles. But the perception of darkness and lightness is not like seeing the son of Diares. Being the son of Diares does not enable his paleness to be seen and is not moved by it, the way that light enables the seeing of colors and is moved by them. So these visibilia distinct from the colors are uncomfortably classified as incidental sensibles. At the very least, they are unlike the only example of incidental sensibles that Aristotle provides us.

Darkness and lightness seem to find no place in the taxonomy of sensibilia provided by \emph{De anima} 2.7. That would make sense if that taxonomy were of the objects of specifically seeing. But once we allow that there are exercises of \emph{opsis} that are not seeings, once we allow that perceptual powers are multi-track, then a taxonomy of objects potentially seen need not apply to the objects of exercises of \emph{opsis} that are not seeings.

(d) \emph{Under conditions of excessive darkness, we perceive by \emph{opsis} that we do not see color, and under normal illumination, we perceive by \emph{opsis} light, a sensible distinct from color.} This is a hybrid interpretation, including, as it does, the claim about darkness perception in (a) with the claim about lightness perception in (c). Ps-Simplicius offers a such a hybrid interpretation (\emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 189 13–28). Concerning lightness perception, Ps-Simplicius writes ``sight does not only perceive colours that have light falling on them, but also perceives light on its own (for we see air when it is lit)'' (\emph{In libros Aristotelis de anima commentaria} 189 19–21, \citealt[42]{Blumenthal:2000rf}). And concerning when we try and fail to see the colored scene, due to excessive darkness say, Ps-Simplicius writes:
\begin{quote}
	[I]t perceives itself, both not seeing anything and seeing; it perceives itself acting, clearly, when it sees, and at the same time it has simultaneous perceptual awareness of its own activity, and in the case when it does not see it perceives itself being not entirely inactive (otherwise it would not be simultaneously aware because awareness is in addition to some activity), but is trying to see, and in trying it is in act not in respect of the seeing but of the trying, so that the awareness is not of seeing, but of the trying which is, so to speak, failing: so the sense of sight makes the judgement that it is not seeing. (Ps-Simplicius, \emph{In libros Aristotelis de anima commentaria} 189 23–8, \citealt[42]{Blumenthal:2000rf})
\end{quote}
Ps-Simplicius has an important insight, here: that the failure to see the colored scene under conditions of excessive darkness remains, nonetheless, the exercise of sight when we experience the obscuring darkness. (We have seen Alexander and Themistius make similar claims.) Sight is not entirely inactive. It is active with respect to the perceiving the surrounding darkness. The qualification, ``entirely'', can be read as signalling that while under conditions of excessive darkness sight is active insofar as we experience the darkness, it is at least not active with respect to seeing the colored scene before one. On the hybrid interpretation, the qualification, ``though not in the same way'', can be read as marking a double distinction. Darkness perception as understood by (a) is contrasted with lightness perception as understood by (c), and both are contrasted with seeing color. On the hybrid interpretation, \citet[275]{Ross:1961uq}, then, is only partially right.

Let us return to the \emph{aporia} set out in \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}. If we perceive by \emph{opsis} that which sees, and perceiving by \emph{opsis} is seeing, and seeing is of color or colored things, then that which sees must itself be colored. But, if we can perceive by \emph{opsis} in a way that is not seeing, and if, more specifically, we can perceive by \emph{opsis} that which sees without seeing it, then no commitment to it being colored is incurred. Something need not be colored to be perceptible by \emph{opsis}, but the more general claim that for a power to be of something it must have a certain nature or being may yet be true. It is just that the way something has to be to be perceived by \emph{opsis} is different from the way something has to be to be seen.

When one inquires into what way something must be to be perceived by \emph{opsis}, a complication is encountered. Perhaps perceiving by \emph{opsis} is itself no single thing. Consider the alternatives that competing interpretations of this notion put forward. Perceiving light in addition to the colored scene that it illuminates seems different in kind from the visual experience that results from trying and failing to see the colored scene under conditions of excessive darkness or brightness. If perceiving by \emph{opsis} is no single thing, then there will be no single nature or being that must be shared by all. 

A further reason for thinking that perceiving by \emph{opsis} is no single thing is the contrast between darkness and lightness perception, on the one hand, and the reflexive case—that we can perceive by \emph{opsis} that which sees. It is unlikely that that which sees shares a nature or being with light and dark, the way it would have to if perceiving by \emph{opsis} were a single thing. A worry arises at this point. While the inference from perceiving by \emph{opsis} that we see to our seeing that we see may have been blocked, why think that we perceive by \emph{opsis} that we see (\citealt[122]{Hamlyn:1961ys} and \citealt[203]{Corkum:2010fa}) and what nature or being must seeing have that makes this possible?  

Alexander of Aphrodisias makes a suggestion concerning the relevant nature or being in the reflexive case:
\begin{quote}
	[Since] these things are in this way, and sensation in actuality comes about by the reception of sensed forms without their matter, it is reasonable that, simultaneously with sensation of the things sensed, we also come to have self-awareness that we are sensing [them, and do so] in respect of the same sense. For it is by receiving the form from the things sensed, which are outside, that we apprehend them, but it is by the sense having the form of the things sened in itself, and [its] having sensation on account of this, and in a way \emph{of} this, that self-awareness [comes about]. (Alexander of Aphrodisias, \emph{Quaestio} 3.7 10–16; \citealt[56]{Sharples:1994aa}) 
\end{quote}
According to Alexander, to perceive by \emph{opsis} that we see, that seeing must have a certain nature or being, that it is a seeing of what is seen. More specifically, it is the exercise of the power of sight that is (1) the reception of the form of the external body, and (2) sensation on account of this, and (3) in a way of this that is the nature or being that a perceptual episode must have in order for the seer to be aware of their seeing what they do, where such awareness is a reflexive self-awareness. 

Let us consider these in turn.

(1) Sight is a multi-track power. There are a plurality of exercises of that power. Seeing is one among the many exercises of the power of sight (if an exemplary one, as sight is for the sake of seeing, \emph{Metaphysica} \( \Theta \) 8 1050a10; seeing being the activity that defines what the power of sight is). Suppose the perceiver is seeing a white thing. To see a white thing is to receive its chromatic form without its matter. But what receives that form? There are three alternatives:
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
	\item The sense organ
	\item The exercise of the power of sight
	\item The power of sight
\end{enumerate}
These alternatives need not be mutually exclusive. Aristotle in \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b22–5) will explicitly endorse (a). The sense organ, the eyes, or more specifically, the transparent water within the eyes, receives the form of whiteness from the external white thing, in the same way that the external transparent medium does, which is different from the way that whiteness inheres in the white thing seen. But that is seemingly consistent with (b) the exercise of the power or (c) the power itself also receiving, as well, the form without the matter of the white thing.

Are there reasons to prefer one of these alternatives, (b) or (c), over the other?

\citet{Johansen:2005hz} argues for (c). In seeing a white thing, the perceiver's power of sight receives the form of whiteness. But this is implausible. First, the reception of form without matter is a description of at least an aspect of an exercise of the power of sight, and not the power itself. The power receives the chromatic form only in the sense that its exercise is the assimilation of that form and is the realization and fulfillment of that power. Moreover, Aristotle seems to accept claims inconsistent with the power of sight in and of itself receiving the form of whiteness. 

Begin by considering the \emph{endoxa}, more specifically the \emph{Timaeus}. According to Timaeus, the Receptacle, the Nurse of Becoming, is free from all the qualities so that it is capable of receiving these from elsewhere:
\begin{quote}
	For if it were like any one of the things that come in upon it then, when things of contrary or entirely different nature came, in receiving them it would reproduce them badly intruding its own features alongside. (\emph{Timaeus} 50e1-4; \citealt[186]{Cornford:1935fk})
\end{quote}
Archer-Hind observes that Aristotle draws upon this passage in his account of \emph{nous} (\emph{De anima} 3.4 429a15–23; \citealt[177 n18]{Archer-Hind:1888qd}, for discussion see \citealt[327–8]{Taylor:1928qb}). The noetic power to receive all things (all intelligible forms) precludes that power from actually being some way for the general reasons given by Timaeus. Moreover, as \citet[327–8]{Taylor:1928qb} observes, there is reason to think that this same theme emerges earlier in \emph{De anima} with the discussion of the perceptual powers. The exercise of a perceptual power may actually be some way by receiving the sensible form of the perceived body, but the power is not and does not, thus retaining its potential to receive contrary sensible qualities and its potential to receive sensible qualities without distortion.

(2) claims that the assimilation of the sensible form of an external body is not sufficient, since familiar forms of natural causation among inanimate bodies, such as fire heating a pot of water, themselves involve the assimilation of form. The water is cool but potentially hot and becomes actually hot when it assimilates the heat of the fire. So it is not just any assimilation of form from an external body. It is the kind of assimilation of form that only sentient living beings are capable of (discussed further in section~\ref{sub:the_argument_from_residual_imagery}). Unlike the power to become hot, the power to assimilate the form without the matter of the perceived object is, not a natural, but a psychic power. But, in assimilating the form of an external body, only sentient living beings have sensation or perception on account of it. So not only is it a vital power, but this vital power is within the provenance of the animal (since the power of perception is exclusive to animals as opposed to plants).

(3) emphasizes the intentionality of perception. In receiving the sensible form of the external body, and having a perception on account of this, that perception is of the external body.

Putting these together, the nature or being that the exercise of the power of sight must have if it is to be a seeing of which one could be aware from within is an assimilation of the sensible form without the matter of the external body, in the way that is distinctive to sensible living beings, such as to give rise to perceptions that are of the external body at least in its sensible respects. Alexander is using a Peripatetic account of perception to pick out from among the plurality of sight's manifestations those that are seeings. Being a seeing is the nature or being of an exercise of sight that is required for reflexive self-awareness of so seeing.

There are stronger and weaker understandings of the Alexandrian position. 

On the stronger reading, the nature or being of the exercise of the power of sight, seeing, is sufficient for that seeing to be perceived by \emph{opsis}. So like Augustine, Peter John Olivi, Descartes, and Sartre, Alexander would be committed to our always perceiving that we see and hear.

On the weaker reading, the nature or being of the exercise of the power of sight, seeing, is an opportunity for that seeing to be perceived by \emph{opsis} (\citealt{Hyman1994-HYMVAP-2}). So like Leibniz, Alexander would only be committed to our sometimes perceiving that we see and hear.

Alexander's endorsement of \textsc{The Regress Argument}, speaks in favor of the stronger reading. An Alexandrian position that drops that endorsement could reasonably embrace the weaker reading.

But that would only help resolve the puzzlement if, more specifically, one can perceive by \emph{opsis} that which sees without seeing it. But nothing so far has been said in support of that claim. To that extent at least, the Argument from Darkness and Light is at best an incomplete resolution of the \emph{aporia}.

\subsection{The Argument from Residual Imagery} % (fold)
\label{sub:the_argument_from_residual_imagery}

\begin{quote}
	\textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery}: Further, that which sees (\emph{to horōn}) is in a sense colored. For each sense organ (\emph{aisthētērion}) receives the sense object without its matter; that is why even after the sense objects have gone, sensations and images remain in the sense organ (\emph{aisthētēríos}). (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b22–5)
\end{quote}

\textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} is Aristotle's second suggestion as to how the \emph{aporia} described in \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} might be resolved. Recall the puzzle was generated by the thought that in order for sight to reflexively apply to itself seeing must itself be colored. Whereas \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} suggests that the reflexive application of sight does not require that seeing be colored, \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} suggests, instead, that \emph{to horōn} is in a sense colored.

Aristotle identifies the seer or that which sees (\emph{to horōn}) with the sense organ (\emph{aisthētērion}). This could only be a synecdoche, the whole standing for the part. For suppose it were not. Then in identifying that which sees with the sense organ, the eyes, it would be the eyes that see, that possess the power of sight, and are the seer in seeing what they do. 

But such a position is criticizable on grounds familiar to Aristotle. Aristotle was a keen student of the \emph{Theaetetus}, and in the \emph{Theaetetus} (184d1–6), Socrates marks a distinction between seeing with and seeing through (\emph{dia}) the eyes. If we see with the eyes, then it is the eyes that see, that possess the power of sight, and are the seer in seeing what they do. And the same holds for the other senses. If we hear with our ears, then it is the ears that hear, that possess the power of hearing, and are the hearer in hearing what they do. By contrast if the animal, and not the eyes, sees, possesses the power of sight, and is the seer. What the animal perceives with is the \emph{psuchē} that animates it. The animal perceives with their \emph{psuchē} through their sense organs, the corporeal instruments of this activity. And this must be the case to explain the unity of sensory consciousness:
\begin{quotation}
	\textsc{Theaetetus}: Well, I should think, Socrates, that it is `\emph{through} which' we perceive in each case, rather than `\emph{with} which.'
	
	\textsc{Socrates}: Yes, my son, it would be a very strange thing, I must say, if there were a number of senses sitting inside us as if we were Wooden Horses, and there were not some single form, soul or whatever one ought to call it, to which all these converge—something \emph{with} which, \emph{through} the senses, as if they were instruments, we perceive all that is perceptible. (\emph{Theaetetus} 184c–d; \citealt[315]{Burnyeat:1990dp})
\end{quotation}

If the seer that becomes in a sense colored is the sense organ, the eyes, and this is not a synecdoche, then we see with the eyes in Socrates's sense. As we shall see (section~\ref{sub:apperceptive_unity}), Aristotle is not insensitive to the unity of consciousness, and would reject seeing with the eyes for Socratic reasons. Indeed, he explicitly alludes to the Achaeans lying in wait in the Wooden Horse in the closing \emph{aporia} about apperceptive unity (\emph{De anima} 3.2 xxx). And so Aristotle would accept that we see through the eyes (even should his full account of how this may be so differ from Socrates'). So \emph{to horōn} must be read as a synecdoche.

It is the sense organ, the eyes, or more specifically, the transparent water within the eyes, that becomes in a sense colored. Moroever, it does so by assimilating the form without the matter of the colored body seen. The qualified attribution of color and the assimilation of form without matter are linked. The way that transparent media within the eyes receives the chromatic form of the body differs from the way that body receives that form. In this way, the transparent water within the eyes is like the transparent external air. Should the body in question, the son of Diares, say, be white, then whiteness is enmattered in the son of Diares in a way that it is not in external and internal transparent media. (In Thomistic vocabulary, the chromatic form has spiritual being in the internal and external transparent media and natural being in the body seen. The spiritual being of color in transparent media is their being in a way colored. And the natural being of color in the body is its possessing that color in the primary way.) 

If \emph{to horōn}, the seer, understood as a synecdoche for the eyes, is in a sense colored, then how does this resolve the \emph{aporia} described in \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}? Recall the crux of the difficulty: ``if one is to see that which sees (\emph{to horōn}), that which sees (\emph{to horōn}) must have color in the primary way''. The formal solution proposed by \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} is that being in a way colored, \emph{to horōn} is not being colored in the primary way. The form of whiteness does not have natural being in \emph{to horōn} in the way that it does in the son of Diares.

Formally, the proposed solution is clear. But it raises a question as to the interpretation of \emph{to horōn}. In \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery}, \emph{to horōn} must be a synecdoche for the sense organ (\emph{aisthētērion}). If the suggested reply is to work—that \emph{to horōn} is colored though not in the primary way—then the occurence of \emph{to horōn} in \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} must receive a coordinate reading, otherwise the reply would be a \emph{non sequitur}. So one might conclude that the occurence of \emph{to horōn}  in \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} must receive \textsc{The Corporeal Reading}. 

The problem is that, on the face of it, this seems wildly implausible. \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} explicitly claims that one sees \emph{to horōn}. But one does not see the eyes that one sees with in being aware from within that one is so seeing. To avoid this bizarre phenomenological claim, and to coordinate with the puzzle in the \emph{Charmides}, \emph{to horōn} must be a synecdoche, not for the eyes, but their characteristic activity, seeing. There, \emph{to horōn} is subject to, not \textsc{The Corporeal Reading}. but \textsc{The Activity Reading}. 

How are we to resolve this hermeneutical conflict?

The exercise of sight in seeing occurs when the sense organ, the eyes, are affected in a certain way. Specifically, the water within the eyes receives the form without the matter of the colored body seen. The water within the eyes are thus in a way colored, but not colored in the primary way. What we are aware of from within is sight's characteristic activity, seeing, where this involves being aware, as well, of the colored body seen. We are manifestly not aware of the state of illumination within the eyes' waters, however. 

Perhaps the subsequent part of the chapter provides a clue. There, Aristotle argues that the activity of the perceived object and the perception are one and the same though they differ in being (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b26–426a27). The activity of the perceived object would be acting in some way, however mediately, to bring about an affection in the sense organ, its reception of the sensible form without the matter of the perceived body. This activity is located where the perceiver is. More specifically, it is localized in the affected sense organ. This affection, the sense organ's assimilation of the form without the matter of the perceived body, brings about the perception of that body. And the activity of the seeing is one and the same as the activity of the colored body seen.

Color is the power to move what is actually transparent. It moves the actually transparent in such a way that the actually transparent assimilates the form without the matter of the colored body seen. The external transparent medium being moved in this way, this motion is communicated to the internal transparent medium of the eyes. It is the assimilation of the form without the matter of colored body by the internal transparent medium that brings about the seeing of the colored body. These are the same activities, located in the perceiver. They are located within the perceiver's eyes, more specifically, they are located where the affected internal water is. The colored body moving the illuminated waters of the eyes and the perceiver's seeing the colored body are one and the same activity though they differ in being. 

There is one activity and two accounts of its being. The accounts need not share vocabulary though they may. But each account must be an account of the same activity. One account is an account of the activity from the perspective of the agent of the activity, whereas the other is an account of the activity from the perspective of the patient.

On \textsc{The Agent Account}, the activity is described in terms of the colored body mediately acting upon the eyes so that the waters within assimilate the body's form without its matter. The activity is described in terms of the agent of the activity, the chromatic power of the visible body to affect the illuminated waters within the eyes.

On \textsc{The Patient Account}, the activity is described as seeing, sight's characteristic activity. Sight's characteristic activity, seeing, is reactive, since sight only sees when the corporeal instruments of sight, the eyes, are acted upon. Sight in seeing only acts by reacting. Sight may be a passive power, its characteristic activity reactive, but it acts in seeing. So the activity is described from the perspective of the agent of the reactive activity, the animal that possesses the passive power of sight.

Whereas \textsc{The Agent Account} is described in purely corporeal terms, it speaks of corporeal powers, and their acting upon a body, \textsc{The Patient Account} is described in psychical terms, it speaks of the characteristic activity of a perceptual power of \emph{psuchē}, that only acts when the sense organ is acted upon. 

The sense organ is animate, and this makes a difference to the effects that acting upon it give rise to. Compare a dead eye and a living eye. Their interior waters may both be illuminated by a chromatic scene before them, but only the living eye thereby sees. The presence and activity of \emph{psuchē} in the living eye, explains the difference in effect. So the corporeal powers in \textsc{The Agent Account} mediately act upon an animate body, the whole and healthy eyes of an awake and attentive animal. It is only with such eyes that the animal will react by seeing the colored body. Moreover, as we shall see, there is a vital power that both animates the eyes and is active in seeing, the common power at work in sight and the other senses. So it is unsurprising that the living eye should cause the animal to react by seeing in the way that the dead eye cannot.

In perceiving by \emph{opsis} that they see, the perceiver is sensitive to an exercise of \emph{opsis}, their seeing a white figure in the distance, by happenstance, the son of Diares. It is perhaps not surprising that the psychic power of sight is sensitive to the exercise of its characteristic activity. (Presumably sight's sensitivity to its inactivity is an effect of sight's sensitivity to its characteristic activity.) Psychic powers are vital powers of living beings whose vital powers and activities are bestowed by an enmattered \emph{psuchē}. They contrast in this way from natural powers, powers that may be possessed by inanimate bodies, in the absence of \emph{psuchē}. 

Sensitivity to its characteristic activity may be a feature of psychic as opposed to natural powers, and then perhaps only among some of them. This sensitivity is manifest in perceiving by \emph{opsis} that one sees. A sensitivity to the characteristic activity of the power of sight, an activity that though located within the eyes, is presented under a certain mode of being—as a reactive activity of the power of sight. Sight's sensitivity is intensional. It is sensitive to the activity as described by \textsc{The Patient Account}. It is, after all, a description of the activity from sight's perspective. Sight's sensitivity to its characteristic activity manifest in its perceiving that it sees, being intensional, is not a sensitivity to the assimilation of form without the matter by the illuminated waters of the eyes. Though the seeing happens there, indeed in the act of assimilation, though the perceiver seeing the colored body and the colored body affecting the eyes may be the same activity, they differ in being nonetheless. That sight's sensitivity to its own activity is intensional explains why, while we may be aware from within that we are seeing, we are manifestly not aware of the state of illumination within the waters of the eyes.


% Sight's sensitivity to its characteristic activity, seeing, presents that activity under a certain mode of being, as its characteristic reactive activity. If so, then in being aware from within that one is seeing a distant white figure moving in the distance, being so aware is not itself an awareness of the state of illumination within the eyes, as if this were somehow visible from within, even if the activity that brings about and sustains that state of illumination is the same activity as sight manifest in seeing the distant white figure—the son of Diares on a late afternoon walk. If sight presents this activity under a mode of being, as its characteristic activity, then this sensitivity does not present the state of illumination within the waters of the eyes, even should this be the very same activity, though differing in being.


That the sense organ receives the form without the matter of the perceived body, receives empirical support: ``even after the sense objects have gone, sensations (\emph{aisthēseis}) and images (\emph{phantasiai}) remain in the sense organ''. \emph{Aisthēsis} is here aptly paraphrased as sensation as opposed to perception since its precisely a case where the sense object has been removed precluding the perceptual construal. What are the \emph{aisthēseis} and \emph{phantasiai} that remain in the sense organ after the sense object has gone?

Before considering that question, first notice that the \emph{aisthēseis} and \emph{phantasiai} are picked out by their etiology. They are the \emph{aisthēseis} and \emph{phantasiai} that remain after the sense object has gone. A colored body, say, moves the actually transparent contiguous with it, which in turn moves the transparent media within the eyes. The \emph{aisthēseis} and \emph{phantasiai} are the effects of this—effects that, in certain circumstances at least, can remain after the colored body is removed from view.

This is important since not all candidates for \emph{aisthēseis} and \emph{phantasiai} satisfy this criterion. Consider Philoponus' suggestion. According to Philoponus (\emph{In De anima} 464,14–7), the \emph{aisthēseis} and \emph{phantasiai} include experiencing ``sparks'' (\emph{marmarugai} or phosphenes in modern parlance). Philoponus will rightly argue that the example, so interpreted, is not fit for purpose. But that should have been evident from the outset. For the experience of sparks is not the after effect of seeing. Thus Aristotle writes
\begin{quote}
	When the eye is pressed and moved, fire seems to flash out. This naturally happens in the dark, or when the eyes are closed; for then, too, there is darkness. (Aristotle, \emph{De sensu} 2 437a24–7; \citealt[219–20]{Hett:1936fk})
\end{quote}

A better example might be sensory memory, recalling the look of something—or its sound, or feel, or odor, or taste—where this involves, as we might put it, the exercise of sensory imagination. A surviving Trojan might recall the look of the Wooden Horse when it first appeared before the gate under a rosy-fingered sky—the vision of the treacherous gift vivid, as the unbidden memory crashes upon him. Our Trojan saw the Wooden Horse in the early morning light, and in so seeing, their sense organ retained the form without the matter of the Wooden Horse which gave rise to a retained \emph{phantasia} which would be later recalled, willingly or no. 

This last example, at least as narrated, does less well with the claim that the \emph{aisthēseis} or \emph{phantasiai} remain specifically in the sense organ. Decades later, sleep disrupted by the vision of the Wooden Horse, the experience of the surviving Trojan is not due to residual motion in the eye's waters, even if the \emph{phantasiai} that haunts his dreams derive from his perception of that accursed gift.

So the relevant \emph{aisthēseis} or \emph{phantasiai} must be the effect of the sensed object and remain in the sense organ. An adequate candidate must satisfy not only the etiological criterion but a criterion of immanence as well—the \emph{aisthēseis} or \emph{phantasiai} must be the result of residual motion within the sense organ. Does anything satisfy these two criteria? 

Perhaps the visual experience of after images does. Looking up from the plain of battle in Asia Minor, an Achaean warrior catches a glimpse of the sun emerging from a cloud of smoke. Momentarily dazzled, they close their eyes and experiences an after image of the sun (\emph{De insomniis} 2 459b11–18). This straightforwardly satisfies the etiological criterion. But a potential empirical problem remains. After images are roughly the complementary color of the body that produced them. If the after image results from assimilating the form without the matter of the colored body seen, then they should be the same color and not a complementary color. 

According to Aristotle, at first the color of the after image is the same as the body seen, but it changes over time until it becomes the complementary color before disappearing: 
\begin{quote}
	And if, after looking at the sun or some other bright object, we shut our eyes, then, if we watch carefully, it appears in the same direct line as we saw it before, first of all in its own proper colour; then it changes to red, and then to purple, until it fades to black and disappears. (Aristotle, \emph{De insomniis} 2 459b13–18; \citealt[355]{Hett:1936fk})
\end{quote}
So the after image is initially white like the sun, but then becomes red, purple, and then black, before disappearing. The initial white appearance is due to assimilating the chromatic form without the matter of the sun. The residual motion or activity within the eyes sustains the initial white appearance of the after image. But this residual activity wanes. And with its waning, the chromatic appearance of the after image changes. This change terminates with the complementary color, black. The after image eventually fades from view when the residual activity ceases.

The after image appers in the same direct line as the bright object even after it has been removed. It is the same direct line from, as we might put it, an ego-centric perspective. Should the bright object appear directly before one, in averting one's gaze, the after image would appear directly before one. No matter where one would look, and even if one closes one's eyes, the after image would appear directly before one. This is reason to think that the after image is not an objective fature of the scene, prior in nature to what is moved. But is rather the upshot of residual activity in the eye having been overstimulated. So not ony do after images satisify the etiological criterion, but the criterion of immanence as well.

The auditory experience of earworms might be another example. Earworms are the effect of hearing a melody that persist even after the melody has stopped playing. They thus satisfy the etiological criterion. And if the sense organ is liberally construed not narrowly as the sensory transducer (the ears including the tympanic membrane and other relevant anatomy) but also including the perceptual system as well (in this case, the auditory system augmented with mnemonic abilities), then perhaps they satsify the criterion of immanence as well, as the melody is retained in a short-term memory buffer. Adhering more closely to Peripatetic anatomy, earworms might be understood as something akin to internal echoes, the motion in the external air echoing in the internal air of the ear. Such internal motions are produced by external motions, and these internal motions might persist after the external motions ceased. So, it would seem that earworms, so understood, would satisfy both the etiological criterion and the criterion of immanence.

\subsection{The Resolutions' Relation} % (fold)
\label{sub:the_resolutions_relation}

\textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} differs from \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light}. Whereas the former insists that \emph{to horōn} is in a sense colored, the latter insists that \emph{to horōn} need not be, sight being a multi-track power. The two suggestions seem to pull in different directions, and a doubt may be raised about their coherence. How are the resolutions related?

\textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} and \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} are either suggestions about how to resolve different difficulties (as Alexander claims, \emph{Queastiones} 3.7), or suggestions about how to resolve the same difficulty—the \emph{aporia} posed by \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}. If they are suggestions about how to resolve the same difficulty, then either they are inconsistent, or they are consistent. If they are inconsistent, then perhaps, like his teacher Plato, Aristotle is leaving it to the reader to decide. The suggested resolution of the closing \emph{aporia} about apperceptive unity is itself notoriously indeterminate, thus leaving further work of understanding to the reader. But if the suggestions are consistent, a question arises as why there are two resolutions of the \emph{aporia}. Does not one suffice? Perhaps they are not responses to different difficulties, but to different aspects of the same difficulty. Or perhaps the suggestions are only jointly sufficient to resolve the difficulty. So why are there two resolutions of one difficulty?

On the interpretation that we have arrived at, \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} and \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} are suggestions about how to resolve the same difficulty—the \emph{aporia} posed by \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} (\emph{pace} Alexander). According to \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light}, perceiving by \emph{opsis} that which sees is not seeing and so need not be colored, thus blocking the aporetic reasoning of \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}. According to \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery}, that which sees is in a sense colored though not in the primary way, thus avoiding the absurd consequence of the aporetic reasoning of \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}.

Moreover, on the present understanding, the suggestions are consistent. \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} suggests that since the perceiving by \emph{opsis} that which sees is not seeing, then what's perceived by \emph{opsis} need not be colored in the primary way. \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} suggests that that which sees is in a way colored, though not in the primary way. So understood, these suggestions are consistent. They each deny that \emph{to horōn} is colored in the primary way, thus escaping the absurd conclusion of \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}.

Why then offer two suggestions about how to resolve the difficulty? Each denies that \emph{to horōn} is colored in the primary way. They differ only in the basis of this denial. \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} makes the denial, not by denying that seeing is of colored things, or that something must be some way in order for a power to apply to it, but by denying that seeing is the sole manifestation of sight, by denying that sight is a single-track power. Similarly, \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} makes the denial, again, not by denying that seeing is of colored things, or that something must be some way in order for a power to apply to it, but by claiming that \emph{to horōn} is only in a sense colored that contrasts with being colored in the primary way. This adds a further positive thought not present in \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light}, that \emph{to horōn} is in a sense colored, by assimilating the form without the matter of the colored body seen. The suggestions goes beyond a simple denial of a premise of \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}. The basis of these denials differ. And so they make different positive claims—that sight is a multi-track power and that the sense organ assimilates a body's form without its matter when perceiving that body.

\section{Potential Objections} % (fold)
\label{sec:potential_objections}

Against the Alexandrian interpretation, \textsc{The Duplication Argument} and the \textsc{The Regress Argument} are not arguments against the higher-order alternative, where in perceiving that we see, seeing is the object of some other \emph{aisthēsis}. Rather, their conclusions are disjunctive, establishing what must be true on each of the alternatives. So \textsc{The Duplication Argument} establishes that transparency or transitivity must hold of the higher-order and reflexive alternatives. And \textsc{The Regress Argument} establishes that reflexivity is a commitment of both alternatives, at least properly developed. The latter highlights the importance of the \emph{aporia} of \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}, derived from the \emph{Charmides}, for that is precisely a puzzle about the commitment to reflexivity shared by the higher-order and reflexive alternatives. Moreover, \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} and \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} are arguments targeting a key claim in generating the \emph{aporia}. Though each evade the difficulty by denying the same claim, they do so on different bases, that sight is a multi-track power and that that which sees is in a way colored that contrasts with being colored in the primary way.

Should \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} and \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} be deemed sound, at least upon suitable development, then the puzzle about reflexivity is resolved. And since the higher-order alternative must accept reflexive perception at some finite level on pain of incoherence, this at the very least speaks strongly in favor of the reflexive alternative, that it is by \emph{opsis} that one perceives that one sees.

There is an open question and potential objections. Moroever, the open question may be closed by careful examination of the potential objections.

The open question concerns the relation between Aristotle's account and the \emph{aporia} of the \emph{Charmides}. Recall there were two ways forward (chapter~\ref{sec:coda}). Given the aporia, one could either hang onto intentionality and reject reflexivity or hang onto reflexivity and reject intentionality. Does Aristotle, in retaining reflexivity, thereby reject intentionality as well, making the account of \emph{De anima} 3.2 an ancient precursor to Sartrean non-positional self-consciousness? Or has Aristotle found another way forward? If so, what way is that?

The potential problems all consist in what appear to be explicit denials of reflexivity. Thus Aristotle writes:
\begin{quote}
	\ldots it is not by sight that one is aware that one sees. (Aristotle, \emph{De somno} 2 455a18; \citealt[327]{Hett:1936fk})
\end{quote}
And Aristotle also writes:
\begin{quote}
	For perception is surely not the perception of itself. (Aristotle, \emph{Metaphysica} {\sbl Γ} 1010b 35; )
\end{quote}
What are we to make of these striking denials? As we shall see, context matters, and matters in a way relevant to our open question.

The \emph{De somno} denial is perhaps the most challenging. The opening and closing \emph{aporiai} of \emph{De anima} 3.2, about perceptual self-consciousness and apperceptive unity respectively, are linked in the \emph{De somno} discussion by being subject to a common explanation. And the denial occurs within this context. If the \emph{De somno} discussion is a consistent extension of the \emph{De anima} account, then Aristotle cannot coherently be read as defending the reflexive alternative in the opening \emph{aporia} of \emph{De anima} 3.2.

Let us consider, then, the \emph{De somno} and \emph{Metaphysica} denials in turn.

\subsection{Apperceptive Unity} % (fold)
\label{sub:apperceptive_unity}

\emph{De anima} 3.2 opens and closes with \emph{aporiai}. Whereas the opening \emph{aporia} concerns perceptual self-consciousness, the closing \emph{aporia} concerns apperceptive unity. The opening \emph{aporia} concerns the possibility of perceiving that we see and hear, while the closing \emph{aporia} concerns perceptually discriminating white from sweet. How is it possible that to perceptually discriminate white from sweet, when these sensible qualities are perceived by distinct senses, sight and taste respectively?

\subsection{\emph{En parergo}} % (fold)
\label{sub:_emph_en_parergo}

Opponents of perceptual reflexivity like to cite the following passage for obvious reasons:
\begin{quote}
 	For perception is surely not the perception of itself, but there is something beyond the perception, which must be prior to the perception. For that which moves is prior in nature to that which is moved. (Aristotle, \emph{Metaphysica} {\sbl Γ} 1010b 35-6)
\end{quote}
After all, does not the very first clause consist in a straightforward denial of reflexivity? 

Bracket, for a moment, the proper interpretation of that clause. Begin, instead, with what else is claimed in the passage. Like Socrates (not only in the \emph{Charmides} but also in the \emph{Gorgias}, for a recent comparison see \citealt[chapter 2]{Moss:2021aa}), Aristotle maintains that the object of perception is distinct from the perceiving of it. In Sartre's terminology, the intentional object is transcendent in the sense that it goes beyond the conscious act directed upon it. For Aristotle, in the case of perception, this follows from the causal role of the object of perception which acts upon the perceiver's sense organ and so must be distinct and prior in nature to that perception. Aristotle, here, makes explicit the tension between intentionality and reflexivity first raised in the \emph{Charmides}. If the object of perception is transcendent, then perception cannot be of itself, at least if that involves reflexive intentionality.

Does Aristotle reject reflexivity then? A qualification complicates matters:
\begin{quote}
 	It seems that knowledge and perception and opinion and understanding have always something else as their object, but themselves only by the side (\emph{en parergō}). (Aristotle, \emph{Metaphysica} {\sbl Μ} 1074b 35–6)
\end{quote}
Aristotle thus allows that intentional psychic activities, while of other things, to be of themselves as well, though only \emph{en parergō}. The qualifier, \emph{en parergō}, suggests a difference in perspective or orientation. Whereas as intentional psychic activities—such as knowing, perceiving, opining, and understanding—are of other things, they may be of themselves, but if they are, only by the side. Compare directly looking at something with something catching your attention in your peripheral vision or, better yet, being aware of something that you are not directly attending to. There are at least two ways to understand this. The first alternative distinguishes species of intentional objects: the primary intentional object is other than the psychic activity directed upon it, while the secondary intentional object may be reflexive. Awareness of the secondary intentional object, by contrast with the primary object, is only ever by the side. On the second alternative, What one is aware of \emph{en parergō} is not a species of intentional object—a secondary intentional object—but is no intentional object all.

According to Brentano, at least as interpreted by \citet{Textor:2017aa}, perception takes as its primary intentional object some other thing and takes as its secondary intentional object that very perception but only ever by the side (\emph{en parergō}). Moreover the specific difference that distinguishes primary and secondary intentional objects is the difference in perspective or orientation signaled by the qualifier \emph{en parergō}. On this first alternative, reflexive intentionality is a component of self-conscious perception. 

The second alternative, however, interprets \emph{en parergō} differently. What one is aware of \emph{en parergō} is not a species of intentional object—a secondary intentional object—but is no intentional object all. Retaining the Socratic thought, of which the subsequent phenomenological tradition makes much, that an intentional object must transcend the perceptual activity directed upon it, and so must be other than that activity, what is perceived \emph{en parergō} is not an intentional object, being reflexive, and so is a form of what Sartre will later describe as non-positional consciousness. On this alternative, the difference in perspective or orientation suffices for this difference in kind. Intentional psychic activities are irreflexive and so reflexive psychic activities must be non-intentional. 

In short, the question dividing these interpretations is whether \emph{en parergō} marks a difference in species or a difference in fundamental kind. And this in turn depends upon how to best understand the difference in orientation or perspective signalled by the qualifier, \emph{en parergō}.
