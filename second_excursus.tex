\section{The First-Person Perspective} % (fold)
\label{sec:first-person_perspective_and_the_phenomenal}

We have seen, in \emph{De somno}, perceptual self-consciousness and apperceptive unity are linked in that they admit of a common explanation. It is the common power (\emph{koinē dunamis}) that explains that we perceive that we see and hear and that we can discriminate white from sweet. A question arises why these seemingly distinct phenomena, perceptual self-consciousness and apperceptive unity, might be linked? We can ask this question both of Aristotle's account in \emph{De somno} but also of ourselves.

Asked of the \emph{De somno} account, the answer seems reasonably clear, at least if we accept that account on its own terms. When the animal sleeps, its power to perceive that it sees and its power to discriminate white from sweet are incapacitated. When asleep, the animal is not aware of seeing the surrounding darkness, and not just because it is not seeing, the animal is not aware of not seeing. What would furnish the animal with this latter awareness is sight itself which is temporarily incapacitated in slumber. Nor, when asleep, can the animal discriminate sensibles from distinct \emph{genera}, and not just because the animal is not perceiving through the the relevant senses, but because the power to discriminate sensibles from distinct \emph{genera} is incapacitated in slumber. It is because the same power is incapacitated, the \emph{koinē dunamis}, when its corporeal instrument is at rest, the primary sense organ (presumably the heart, given Aristotle's cardiocentrism) and because this power is multi-track, being manifest both in perceptual self-consciousness and apperceptive unity, that these distinct phenomena are related.

If we ask the same question of ourselves, rather than specifically of Aristotle's \emph{De somno} account, a deeper answer emerges, and one that mays nonetheless be hovering in the background of Aristotle's own reflection in \emph{De somno} and other works. So why might perceptual self-consciousness and apperceptive unity be linked in the way Aristotle suggests? On the interpretation advanced in the last chapter, perceptual self-consciousness is reflexive in character rather than being higher-order. Here, then, is the suggestion: Reflexivity and unity are each essential components of the first-person perspective.

\textsc{The Regress Argument}, if it is not simply invalid \citep[202]{Corkum:2010fa}, must involve the assumption that every perception is conscious as Themistius (\emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima paraphrasis}
83,15–6) suggests and that the avowed Cartesian Sartre (\emph{L'Être et le Néant}) insists upon. If consciousness at least involves being aware from within that one perceives, and such awareness is reflexive, then having a conscious first-person perspective essentially invovles a reflexive component. Even if, like Leibniz (\emph{Principes de la Nature et de la Grâce} §4, \emph{La Monadologie} §14), one insists upon distinguishing perception and apperception thus countenancing unconscious perception, one might still think that it is essential to the first-person perspective that one might apperceive. The difference is that the Cartesian position insists that one necessarily enjoys reflexive awareness whenever one perceives, whereas the Leibnizian position insists that at most some perceivers must be capable of reflexive awareness, whether or not that power is exercised.

A conscious first-person perspective is reflexive. Apollo's perspective on the Achaean encampment is his own. It is a perspective from here, where Apollo himself is located, and directed upon the Achaean encampment below. And it is a perspective Apollo now enjoys as he stretches his bow against the Achaeans.

A conscious first-person perspective is unified. It is because the animal's first-person perspective is unified can that animal discriminate white from sweet. This discrimination is the act of a single agent. Without the requisite unity, the senses would be like so many separate Achaeans hiding in the Wooden Horse (\emph{Theaetetus} 184c–d; chapter~\ref{sub:the_argument_from_residual_imagery}). One sense may perceive white and another sweet, but neither sense, nor even these senses in combination, would be able to discriminate white from sweet. Such discriminatory activity is only possible from a unified perspective. It is not just our ability to discriminate sensibles from distinct \emph{genera} that would be compromised, but much more besides. Consider Augustine's description of responding to bodily injury:
\begin{quote}
	When, for instance, there is an ache in the foot, the eye looks at it, the mouth speaks of it, and the hand reaches for it. (Augustine, \emph{De immortalitate animae} 16.25; \citealt[46]{Schopp:1947df})
\end{quote}
The coordinated activities of the eye, mouth, and hand simultaneously directed at the injured foot would only be possible from the unified first-person perspective afforded by ``some single form, soul or whatever one ought to call it'' as Socrates puts it (Plato, \emph{Theaetetus} 184d; \citealt[315]{Burnyeat:1990dp}).

\section{\emph{Sentio Ergo Sum}} % (fold)
\label{sec:sensato_ergo_sum}

Aristotle taxonmizes living beings by their psychic powers. All living beings possess the powers of nutrition, growth, and reproduction. Plants, no less than animals, engage in these vital activities. But what distinguishes plants from animals is that animals have, in addition, perceptual powers. And what distinguishes humans from non-human animals is their possession of reason. The perceptual powers that distinguish animals from plants include not only the special senses, but the common power that animates them as well as related, dependent powers such as \emph{phantasia} and memory. Since the common power is not the sole provenance of humanity, and the common power is responsible for reflexive awareness, being aware from within that one is seeing the scene before one is possible for the experience of an animal and not, specifically, a human animal.

Let us pause and consider the anti-Cartesian significance of this.

Begin by considering a striking claim in \emph{De sensu} delivered in the course of an argument that there is no imperceptible time:
\begin{quote}
	For if, when a man perceives himself or anything else in continuous time, it is impossible for him to be unaware of his existence, and if again in continuous time there can be a time so short as to be quite imperceptible, it is clear that, during that time, he would be unaware of his existence, and of the fact of his seeing or perceiving. (Aristotle, \emph{De sensu} 7 448a26–8; \citealt[277]{Hett:1936fk})
\end{quote}
In his \emph{De sensu} commentary, Alexander summarizes the claim as follows:
\begin{quote}
	[I]f, when someone perceives himself perceiving and [does so] in time, it is impossible for it at that time to escape detection by him that he exists and perceives. (Alexander of Aphrodisias, \emph{In librum De sensu commentarium} 7 147,25–7; \citealt[134]{Towey:2000qn})
\end{quote}
Hett translates \emph{mē\ldots lanthanein} as not unaware. Originally, \emph{lanthanein} means hidden or covered and comes to have the psychic sense of unnoticed. With the negation \emph{mē}, it means not unnoticed. Within the Platonic tradition this roundabout expression is used for awareness (for example, in Plato \emph{Philebus} 33d, Plotinus \emph{Enneads} 4.4 18–19). This usage survives in Latin as \emph{non latere}. Augustine, no doubt influened by the books of the Platonists (\emph{Confessiones} 7.9.13), uses \emph{non latere animum}, not hidden from the soul, to mean awareness (for example, Augustine, \emph{De quantitate animae} 23.41; for discussion see \citealt[86 n15]{ODaly:1987fq}, \citealt[275 n61]{Brittain:2002hl}, \citealt[chapter 12]{Sorabji:2006aa}, \citealt[14–5]{Kalderon:2021xs}). If, as Hett's translation implicitly suggests, Aristotle is here picking up on and deploying this Platonic usage, then \emph{mē\ldots lanthanein} designates awareness that may be more straightforwardly expressed the verb \emph{aisthanomai}. And if that is right, it is coordinated with the language of the opening \emph{aporia} of \emph{De anima} 3.2.

In perceiving, the animal's existence does not go unnoticed. \citet{Brehier:1942dl} describes this as an archaic form of the \emph{Cogito}. And \citet[361–2]{Mondolfo:1953ce}, punning on the Cartesian \emph{Cogito}, encapsulates the key thought of this passage with the slogan \emph{Sentio ergo sum}. (For discussion of this passage, \emph{De sensu} 7 448a26–8, see \citealt[chapter 5]{Heller-Roazen:2007ee}.)

Mondolfo's pun is deliberate and philosophically significant. For it draws our attention to a salient difference between Aristotle and Descartes. Whereas the Cartesian \emph{Cogito} derives from cognition, the Peripatetic \emph{Sentio} derives from perception. This can be obscured, somewhat, by the fact that Descartes assimilates perception to cognition in a way that Aristotle declines to do, but the difference remains real. Moreover, for Descartes, cognition is the provenance of the human. Non-human animals lack souls, and hence cognition, and their behavior is explicable in purely mechanistic terms. A non-human animal is but a \emph{b\^{e}te-machine}. Aristotle, however, understands \emph{psuchē} as the principle of life, in a way that Descartes declines to do, vital activity being subject to a purely mechanical explanation. Perhaps relevant, here, is Descartes fascination with the Strasbourg astronomical clock and automata more generally. In such cases, complex behavior is determined by an unseen internal mechanism making plausible to Descartes that vital activity generally may too be mechanically explicable. Descartes' fascination was so great that it gave rise to the apocryphal story that after the death of his daughter Francine of scarlet fever in 1640, Descartes had an animatronic effigy of her constructed, ultimately thrown overboard when travelling by sea by the terrified captain (a tale first reported by Bonaventure d’Argonne in \emph{M\'{e}langes d'histoire et de litt\'{e}rature}, see \citealt{Kang:2017tb}). \emph{Contra} Descartes, Aristotle maintaines that since non-human animals live, they are ensouled. Non-human animals may lack reason, but they share with humans the power to perceive. And if they share with humans the power to perceive, they share with humans the power to apperceive.

This has been interpreted as a summary statement of Descartes' view, at least as standardly interpreted.
