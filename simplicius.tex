\section{Philoponus' Challenge} % (fold)
\label{sec:philoponus_challenge}

\begin{quote}
	Besides, it is absurd that the same sense should know that it sees. For it must be reflecting back on itself after having seen the colour that it gets to know that it sees. But if it reflects on itself it also has an activity which is separate, and what has a sperate activity has a separate substance, and on that account is eternal and incorporeal. So someone who says in an ambiguous way that the rational soul is immortal will be shouting out plainly that the non-rational soul is immortal; which is absurd. The senses are not eternal, and for that reason do not reflect on themselves. And if they do not reflect on themselves they do not lay hold of their own activities. For a thing's reflecting on itself is nothing other than the laying hold of itself. (Philoponus)
\end{quote}

It is absurd that the same sense should know that it perceives. This is less a rejection of reflexivity than a rejection of an explanation of reflexivity in terms of a sense reverting upon its own activity. Let the sense be sight and its perceiving be seeing. Sight cannot know that it sees. Knowledge, here, need not be discursively articulated the way \emph{epistēmē} is. To know something, in the relevant sense, is to lay hold upon it, to apprehend it—it is mode of awareness. To know that it sees, sight must revert upon its activity, seeing. But this reversion is subsequent to this seeing. Only after having seen the color, sight gets to know that it sees. 

\begin{quotation}
	Prop. 15: \emph{All that is capable of reverting upon itself is incorporeal}
	For it is not in that nature of any body to revert upon itself. That which reverts upon anything is conjoined with that upon which it reverts: hence it is evident that every part of a body reverted upon itself must be conjoined with every other part—since self-reversion is precisely the case in which the reverted subject and that upon which it has reverted become identical. But this is impossible for a body, and universally for any divisible substance: for the whole of a divisible substance cannot be conjoined with the whole of itself, because of the separation of its parts, which occupy different positions in space. It is not in the nature, then, of any body to revert upon itself so that the whole is reverted upon the whole. Thus if there is anything which is capable of reverting upon itself, it is incorporeal and without parts. (Proclus, \emph{Elements of Theology}; \citealt[19]{Dodds:1963ul})
\end{quotation}

\section{Ps-Simplicius} % (fold)
\label{sec:ps_simplicius}

Ps-Philoponus denies that perceiveing that we see and hear is the activity of a perceptual power maintaining, instead, that it is the activity of the attentive part (\emph{tou prosektikou merous}) of reason (\emph{logos}) (\emph{In de anima}, 464,30–465,17). Against this, Ps-Simplicius will insist that while reason may play a role in reflexive awareness, it does so consistent with perceiving that we see and hear being the activity of a perceptual power. Like Priscian, Ps-Simplicius (possibly one and the same) adheres to the Iamblichean doctrine that reason suffuses all things (\emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 173,1–7, 187,35–9; on Priscian's adherence see \citealt[448]{Kalderon:2017rw}, on Ps-Simplicius' see \citealt[332]{Lautner:2014cz}). Reason suffuses even the perceptual powers of the perceiver. But that is consistent with the perceptual powers, so suffused, and not reason, nor a part of it, explaining that we perceive that we see and hear.
