\chapter{Procession and Reversion} % (fold)
\label{cha:procession}

\section{Introduction} % (fold)
\label{sec:introduction}

Ps-Simplicius' commentary on \emph{De anima} 3.2, while presented as a linear commentary, present two notable departures from that genre. 

First, what purports to be a commentary on \textsc{The How-Possible Question} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b12–3) is, in fact, an Iamblachean prologue to the subsequent linear commentary. The Iamblachean prologue states the truth of the matter as revealed by the Divine Iamblichus. Though about the topic of \emph{De anima} 3.2, the truth, at least as revealed by Iamblichus, is stated in non-Peripatetic, indeed, post-Plotinian terms. An alien vocabulary imposes itself on the discussion of perceptual self-consciousness. Sight perceiving that it sees is understood in terms of sight reverting (\emph{epistrophein}) upon its activity.

Second, the linear commentary proper begins with \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b17–20). Ps-Simplicius passess over \textsc{The Duplication Argument} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 4225b13–15) and \textsc{The Regress Argument} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b15-7). What explains this apparent indifference is that Ps-Simplicius believes that \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} is the central \emph{aporia} of the opening aporetic reasoning, indeed the only part of that reasoning that is explicitly described as an \emph{aporia}. So Ps-Simplicius' linear commentary only covers the puzzle, \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}, and the two suggested resolutions of that puzzle, \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b20–2) and \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b22–5). It is this incompleteness that constitutes the departure from the genre of linear commentary.

\section{Ps-Philoponus' Challenge} % (fold)
\label{sec:philoponus_challenge}

\begin{quote}
	Besides, it is absurd that the same sense should know that it sees. For it must be reflecting back on itself after having seen the colour that it gets to know that it sees. But if it reflects on itself it also has an activity which is seperate, and what has a seperate activity has a seperate substance, and on that account is eternal and incorporeal. So someone who says in an ambiguous way that the rational soul is immortal will be shouting out plainly that the non-rational soul is immortal; which is absurd. The senses are not eternal, and for that reason do not reflect on themselves. And if they do not reflect on themselves they do not lay hold of their own activities. For a thing's reflecting on itself is nothing other than the laying hold of itself. (Ps-Philoponus, \emph{In De anima} 466,18-27; \citealt[]{Charlton:2013aa})
\end{quote}

The overall structure of the reasoning here is tolerably clear, at least in outline. It is absurd that the same sense should know that it perceives. This is less a rejection of reflexivity \emph{per se} than a rejection of an explanation of reflexivity in terms of a sense reverting upon its own activity. Let the sense be sight, and its perceiving be seeing. Sight could not know that it sees. Knowledge, here, need not be discursively articulated the way \emph{epistēmē} is. To know something, in the relevant sense, is to lay hold upon it, to apprehend it—it is mode of awareness. Thus, to know, in this sense, that it sees, sight would have to revert upon its activity, seeing. But what is able to revert upon itself is able to act separately from the body (\emph{chōristēs estin energeias}). And what acts seperately from the body is eternal and incorporeal. This has the consequence that the sight, and the senses more generally, would be eternal and incorporeal. But that is absurd. And so Ps-Philoponus concludes, by \emph{reductio}, that the senses do not revert upon their own activity. (For discussion see \citealt[158]{Bernard:1987hc}.)

There are questions both about the target of this criticism, and the strength of its premises.

First, the reversion account, as reported by Ps-Philoponus, is phenomenologically inapt, at least if the temporal language of the passage is read literally: ``For it must be reflecting back on itself after having seen the colour that it gets to know that it sees.'' Sight's seeing and sight's reversion upon its seeing are termporally separated. First one sees, and only then does sight know that it sees in a subsequent act of reversion. Understood literally, what Ps-Philoponus reports is a better account of sensory recall than perceptual self-consciousness. But seeing and the awareness of so seeing are simultaneous, occurring as they do in non-seperate time. 

Moreover, talk of reversion by itself does not foist this commitment upon one. If Philoponus is accurately reporting the temporal language of his target, he is uncharitably taking such language literally. Consider, for example, Plotinus' narrative of ontogenesis in the \emph{Fifth Ennead}. The Incohate Intellect proceeds from the One, given the overflowing abundant good of the One. The Incohate Intellect, despairing of its separation from the One, attempts to reunite with it. And so, in an act of reversion, it turns and looks and sees only itself, thus constituting itself as the Intellect, a fragmentary image of the hyperontic One. In the narrative, procession precedes reversion. But the narrative here is clearly mythic. The relevant procession and reversion are co-eternal activities and do not occur in seperate time. There may, of course, be differences between procession and reversion in the intelligible and sensible realms. But these differences must be specified and shown to sufficiently justify the claim that within the sensible realm, procession and reversion must occur in seperate time. Indeed, as we shall see (section~\ref{sec:ps_simplicius}), Ps-Simplicius, in his own development of the reversion account, emphasizes just such a difference (\emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 187,38–188,3) but where the act of reversion and the perceptual activity that it reverts upon occur simulateneously.

Second, the strength of Ps-Philoponus' premises seem unduly strong. As presented, we only get to the conclusion that what is able to revert upon itself is eternal and incorporeal, if the act of reversion is seperate in the strong sense of being wholly seperate from the body. But perhaps reversion in the sensible realm is seperate if not wholly seperate \citep[158]{Bernard:1987hc}. If possible at all, awareness of perceptual activity by sight's reversion upon it could not be wholly seperate from the body since sight's activity in seeing involves the activity of its corporeal instruments, the eyes. Again, as we shall see (section~\ref{sec:ps_simplicius}), Ps-Simplicius, in his own development of the reversion account, maintians that sight does not wholy revert upon its characteristic activity.

\section{Proclus} % (fold)
\label{sec:proclus}

Proclus is in the tradition that Ps-Philoponus is criticizing, whether or not he expliclty has him in mind in this passage. Relevant, here, are \textsc{Propositions} 15 (all that is capable of reverting upon itself is incorporeal) and 16 () of \emph{Elementatio Theologica}. There are differences, however. Whereas Ps-Philoponus represents reasoning from separateness to incorporeality, Proclus, in \textsc{Proposition} 16, represents separateness as a consequence of incorporeality. (Though distinct lines of reasoning, they may be consistent should separateness and incorporeality prove mutually entailing.)

\begin{quote}
	\textsc{Proposition} 15: \emph{All that is capable of reverting upon itself is incorporeal.} For it is not in that nature of any body to revert upon itself. That which reverts upon anything is conjoined with that upon which it reverts: hence it is evident that every part of a body reverted upon itself must be conjoined with every other part—since self-reversion is precisely the case in which the reverted subject and that upon which it has reverted become identical. But this is impossible for a body, and universally for any divisible substance: for the whole of a divisible substance cannot be conjoined with the whole of itself, because of the separation of its parts, which occupy different positions in space. It is not in the nature, then, of any body to revert upon itself so that the whole is reverted upon the whole. Thus if there is anything which is capable of reverting upon itself, it is incorporeal and without parts. (Proclus, \emph{Elementatio Theologica}; \citealt[19]{Dodds:1963ul})
\end{quote}

\section{Ps-Simplicius} % (fold)
\label{sec:ps_simplicius}

Ps-Philoponus denies that perceiveing that we see and hear is the activity of a perceptual power maintaining, instead, that it is the activity of the attentive part (\emph{tou prosektikou merous}) of reason (\emph{logos}) (\emph{In de anima}, 464,30–465,17). Against this, Ps-Simplicius will insist that while reason may play a role in reflexive awareness, it does so consistent with perceiving that we see and hear being the activity of a perceptual power. Like Priscian, Ps-Simplicius (possibly one and the same) adheres to the Iamblichean doctrine that reason suffuses all things (\emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 173,1–7, 187,35–9; on Priscian's adherence see \citealt[448]{Kalderon:2017rw}, on Ps-Simplicius' see \citealt[332]{Lautner:2014cz}). Reason suffuses even the perceptual powers of the perceiver. But that is consistent with the perceptual powers, so suffused, and not reason, nor even a part of it, explaining that we perceive that we see and hear.

In what sense does reason suffuse perception consistent with the division between perception and reason? Reason suffuses all things at least in the sense that all things aspire to imitate reason insofar as they can. So our perceptual powers, bound as they are to their coproreal instruments, are limited in the extent to which they may imitate the operation of rational powers. Though limited, they are not utterly impotent in this regard. While reason, being incorporeal, may wholly revert upon itself, perception, being partially corporeal, may only partially revert upon itself. 

Perception is partially corproreal since only animate bodies perceive. The animal only has a special sense if it possesses the relevant corporeal instrument. However, not only must this corporeal instrument be appropriately juxtaposed to the rest of the animal's body, but it must be (1) animated by \emph{psuchē} and (2) integrated into the vital operation of the whole and healthy animal. Given these features over and above the juxtaposition of the corporeal instrument, the exercise of sight in seeing is a psychic activity, an activity of an animate as opposed to an inanimate body.
