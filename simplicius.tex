\chapter{Procession and Reversion} % (fold)
\label{cha:procession}

\section{Philoponus' Challenge} % (fold)
\label{sec:philoponus_challenge}

\begin{quote}
	Besides, it is absurd that the same sense should know that it sees. For it must be reflecting back on itself after having seen the colour that it gets to know that it sees. But if it reflects on itself it also has an activity which is separate, and what has a sperate activity has a separate substance, and on that account is eternal and incorporeal. So someone who says in an ambiguous way that the rational soul is immortal will be shouting out plainly that the non-rational soul is immortal; which is absurd. The senses are not eternal, and for that reason do not reflect on themselves. And if they do not reflect on themselves they do not lay hold of their own activities. For a thing's reflecting on itself is nothing other than the laying hold of itself. (Philoponus)
\end{quote}

The overall structure of the reasoning here is tolerably clear, at least in outline. It is absurd that the same sense should know that it perceives. This is less a rejection of reflexivity \emph{per se} than a rejection of an explanation of reflexivity in terms of a sense reverting upon its own activity. Let the sense be sight, and its perceiving be seeing. Sight could not know that it sees. Knowledge, here, need not be discursively articulated the way \emph{epistēmē} is. To know something, in the relevant sense, is to lay hold upon it, to apprehend it—it is mode of awareness. Thus, to know that it sees, sight would have to revert upon its activity, seeing. But what is able to revert upon itself is able to act separately from the body (\emph{chōristēs estin energeias}). And what acts seperately from the body is eternal and incorporeal. This has the consequence that the sight, and the senses more generally, would be eternal and incorporeal. But this is absurd. And so Ps-Philoponus concludes by \emph{reductio} that the senses do not revert upon their own activity.

There are questions both about the target of this criticism, and the strength of its premises.

First, the reversion account, at least as reported by Ps-Philoponus, is phenomenologically inapt, at least if the temporal language of the passage is read literally: ``For it must be reflecting back on itself after having seen the colour that it gets to know that it sees.'' Sight's seeing and sight's reversion upon its seeing are termporally separated. First one sees, and only then does sight know that it sees in a subsequent act of reversion. But seeing and the awareness of so seeing are simultaneous, occurring as they do in non-separate time. 

Moreover, talk of reversion by itself does not foist this commitment upon one. I suspect that if Philoponus is accurately reporting the temporal language of his target, he is uncharitably taking such language literally. Consider, for example, Plotinus' narrative of ontogenesis in the \emph{Fifth Ennead}. The Incohate Intellect proceeds from the One, given the overflowing abundant good of the One. The Incohate Intellect, despairing of its separation from the One, attempts to reunite with it. In an act of reversion, it turns and looks and sees only itself, thus constituting itself as the Intellect, a fragmentary image of the hyperontic One. In the narrative, procession precedes reversion. But the narrative here is clearly mythic. The relevant procession and reversion are co-eternal activities and do not occur in separate time. There may, of course, be differences between procession and reversion in the intelligible and sensible realms. But these must be specified sufficiently to justify the claim that within the sensible realm, procession and reversion occur separately.

Second, the strength of Ps-Philoponus' premises seem unduly strong. As presented, we only get to the conclusion that what is able to revert upon itself is eternal and incorporeal, if the act of reversion is separate in the strong sense of being completely separate from the body. But perhaps reversion in the sensible realm is separate if not completely separate.

Proclus is at least in the tradition Ps-Philoponus is criticizing, whether he expliclty has him in mind in this passage. Relevant, here, are \textsc{Propositions} 15 and 16 of \emph{Elementatio Theologica}. There are differences. Whereas Ps-Philoponus represents reasoning from separateness to incorporeality, Proclus, in \textsc{Proposition} 16, represents separateness as a consequence of incorporeality. (Though distinct lines of reasoning, they may be consistent should separateness and incorporeality prove mutually entailing.)

\begin{quote}
	\textsc{Proposition} 15: \emph{All that is capable of reverting upon itself is incorporeal}
	For it is not in that nature of any body to revert upon itself. That which reverts upon anything is conjoined with that upon which it reverts: hence it is evident that every part of a body reverted upon itself must be conjoined with every other part—since self-reversion is precisely the case in which the reverted subject and that upon which it has reverted become identical. But this is impossible for a body, and universally for any divisible substance: for the whole of a divisible substance cannot be conjoined with the whole of itself, because of the separation of its parts, which occupy different positions in space. It is not in the nature, then, of any body to revert upon itself so that the whole is reverted upon the whole. Thus if there is anything which is capable of reverting upon itself, it is incorporeal and without parts. (Proclus, \emph{Elementatio Theologica}; \citealt[19]{Dodds:1963ul})
\end{quote}

\section{Ps-Simplicius} % (fold)
\label{sec:ps_simplicius}

Ps-Philoponus denies that perceiveing that we see and hear is the activity of a perceptual power maintaining, instead, that it is the activity of the attentive part (\emph{tou prosektikou merous}) of reason (\emph{logos}) (\emph{In de anima}, 464,30–465,17). Against this, Ps-Simplicius will insist that while reason may play a role in reflexive awareness, it does so consistent with perceiving that we see and hear being the activity of a perceptual power. Like Priscian, Ps-Simplicius (possibly one and the same) adheres to the Iamblichean doctrine that reason suffuses all things (\emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 173,1–7, 187,35–9; on Priscian's adherence see \citealt[448]{Kalderon:2017rw}, on Ps-Simplicius' see \citealt[332]{Lautner:2014cz}). Reason suffuses even the perceptual powers of the perceiver. But that is consistent with the perceptual powers, so suffused, and not reason, nor a part of it, explaining that we perceive that we see and hear.
