\chapter{Procession and Reversion} % (fold)
\label{cha:procession}

\section{Introduction} % (fold)
\label{sec:introduction_pr}

Ps-Simplicius' commentary on \emph{De anima} 3.2, while presented as a linear commentary, present two notable departures from that genre. 

First, what purports to be a commentary on \textsc{The How-Possible Question} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b12–3) is, in fact, an Iamblachean prologue to the subsequent linear commentary. The Iamblachean prologue states the truth of the matter as revealed by the Divine Iamblichus. Though about the topic of \emph{De anima} 3.2, the truth, at least as revealed by Iamblichus, is stated in non-Peripatetic, indeed, Plotinian terms (Plotinus, \emph{Enneads} 4.8.4, 5.2.1, on the relationshipe between Plotinus and Iamblichus see \citealt{Steel:1978th}). An alien vocabulary imposes itself on the discussion of perceptual self-consciousness. Sight perceiving that it sees is understood in terms of sight reverting (\emph{epistrophein}) upon its activity.

Second, the linear commentary proper begins with \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b17–20). Ps-Simplicius passess over \textsc{The Duplication Argument} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 4225b13–15) and \textsc{The Regress Argument} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b15-7) in the linear commentary proper. There is no separate lemmata for these. At most there is a paraphrase of these passages at the end of the Iamblachean prologue. Moreover, the paraphrase is presented in terms of the Iamblachean psychology there presupposed. Unlike in the subsequent linear commentary, there is no textual exegesis, only a summary presentation in an alien vocabulary. What explains this apparent indifference is that Ps-Simplicius believes that \textsc{The Argument from Relatives} is the central \emph{aporia} of the opening aporetic reasoning, indeed the only part of that reasoning that is explicitly described as an \emph{aporia}. So Ps-Simplicius' linear commentary only covers the puzzle, \textsc{The Argument from Relatives}, and the two suggested resolutions of that puzzle, \textsc{The Argument from Darkness and Light} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b20–2) and \textsc{The Argument from Residual Imagery} (\emph{De anima} 3.2 425b22–5). It is this incompleteness that constitutes the second departure from the genre of linear commentary.

In the introduction to his linear commentary, in the course of highlighting a central theme of Book 3 of \emph{De anima} (the soul as a mean between the indivisibilty of intellgience and the divisibility of sensation—a theme perhaps more Timaean than Aristotelian), Ps-Simplicius frames his discussion in an alien vocabulary (\emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 6.6ff). Specifically, he introduces the conceptual triad: rest (\emph{monē}), procession (\emph{proodos}), and reversion (\emph{epistophē}). Procession is a movement toward, an approach (Thucydides, \emph{Xiungraphē peri tou Peloponnesiakou polemou} 4.110), whereas reversion is a turning toward or upon (\emph{Revelation} 1:12). The language is ordinary, though assumes an extrordinary significance in Neoplatonic thought. In general, things begin at rest, proceed outwards, and then revert back into themselves thus abiding in rest and so completing the cycle. Though the conceptual triad is fundamentally metaphysical, essential to the description of the operation of higher causes, it has application to \emph{psuchē} as well. Thus, for example, while Damascius primarily uses these notions in the metaphysical sense (\emph{De principiis} 4 2,2–3, \emph{In Phaedonem} 1 43,3, 62,3–4), they nonetheless find psychological application in explaining self-consciousness in terms of reversion (\emph{In Phaedonem} 1 269,4–6). It is this application of \emph{epistophē} to specifically perceptual self-consciousness that we shall be primarily concerned.

Once the conceptual triad of rest (\emph{monē}), procession (\emph{prohodos}), and reversion (\emph{epistophē}) is established, it is no surprise that reversion should find application in the discussion of self-knowledge and allied topics such as perceiving that we see and hear. However, this intrusion of alien vocabulary into the commentary tradition on \emph{De anima} was not everywhere welcomed. Ps-Philoponus, for one, was a harsh critic.

\section{Ps-Philoponus' Challenge} % (fold)
\label{sec:philoponus_challenge}

At the end of a discussion of previous commentators, including Alexander of Aphrodisias and Plutarch of Athens, Ps-Philoponus crticizes a view recognizably of Neoplatonic origin:
\begin{quote}
	Besides, it is absurd that the same sense should know that it sees. For it must be reflecting back on itself after having seen the colour that it gets to know that it sees. But if it reflects on itself it also has an activity which is separate, and what has a separate activity has a separate substance, and on that account is eternal and incorporeal. So someone who says in an ambiguous way that the rational soul is immortal will be shouting out plainly that the non-rational soul is immortal; which is absurd. The senses are not eternal, and for that reason do not reflect on themselves. And if they do not reflect on themselves they do not lay hold of their own activities. For a thing's reflecting on itself is nothing other than the laying hold of itself. (Ps-Philoponus, \emph{In De anima} 466,18-27; \citealt[]{Charlton:2013aa})
\end{quote}

The overall structure of the reasoning here is tolerably clear, at least in outline. It is absurd that the same sense should know that it perceives. This is less a rejection of reflexivity \emph{per se} than a rejection of an explanation of reflexivity in terms of a sense reverting upon its own activity. Let the sense be sight, and its perceiving be seeing. Sight could not know that it sees. Knowledge, here, need not be discursively articulated the way \emph{epistēmē} is. To know something, in the relevant sense, is to lay hold upon it, to apprehend it—it is mode of awareness. Thus, to know, in this sense, that it sees, sight would have to revert upon its activity, seeing. But what is able to revert upon itself is able to act separately from the body (\emph{chōristēs estin energeias}). And what acts separately from the body is eternal and incorporeal. This has the consequence that the sight, and the senses more generally, would be eternal and incorporeal. But that is absurd. And so Ps-Philoponus concludes, by \emph{reductio}, that the senses do not revert upon their own activity. (For discussion see \citealt[158]{Bernard:1987hc}.)

Ps-Philoponus' argument here is usefully compared to the argument of Porphyry \emph{Sentenciae} 41. Ps-Philoponus accepts that we perceive that we see and hear. Indeed, he explains this in terms of the \emph{prosektikon} theory (discussed in chapter~\ref{sub:the_how_possible_question}). What Ps-Philoponus rejects is the explanation of perceiving that we see and here in terms of sight's reversion upon itself. Porphyry, similarly, accepts that perceiving that we see and hear cannot be explained in terms of reversion if this implies separability from the body:
\begin{quote}
	That which has its being in another and has no essential existence on its own separate from another, if it turns to itself for the purpose of knowing itself, without taking account of that to which it owes is essential existence in withdrawing itself from that, it destroys itself.\ldots If it is the case of sight, and the sense-faculties in general, that none is perceptive of itself, nor can be, if it separates itself from the body, have any consciousness of itself, or even survive\ldots [since] the sense-faculties have aquired their efficacy through the medium of a body. (Porphyry, \emph{Sentenciae} 41; Dillon in \citealt[829]{Brisson:2005qr})
\end{quote}
So according to Porphyry, sight, if it seeks to know itself by turning to itself (\emph{pros heauto epistrephein}) thereby separating itself from the body, destroys itself since the power of sight and its activity depends upon its corporeal instruments, the eyes. Porphyry thus makes a good witness for Ps-Philoponus prosecution of the reversion account of perceptual self-consciousness.

There are questions both about the target of Ps-Philoponus' criticism and the strength of his premises.

First, the reversion account, as reported by Ps-Philoponus, is phenomenologically inapt, at least if the temporal language of the passage is read literally: ``For it must be reflecting back on itself after having seen the colour that it gets to know that it sees.'' Sight's seeing and sight's reversion upon its seeing are termporally separated. First one sees, and only then does sight know that it sees in a subsequent act of reversion. Understood literally, what Ps-Philoponus reports is a better account of \emph{phantasia} or sensory recall involving \emph{phantasia} than perceptual self-consciousness. Whereas the \emph{phantasia} that seeing the Wooden Horse gave rise to is post-perceptual, seeing and the awareness of so seeing are simultaneous, occurring as they do in non-separate time. 

Moreover, talk of reversion by itself does not foist this commitment upon one. If Ps-Philoponus is accurately reporting the temporal language of his target, he is uncharitably taking such language literally. Consider, for example, Plotinus' narrative of ontogenesis in the \emph{Fifth Ennead}. The Incohate Intellect proceeds from the One, given its overflowing abundant goodness. The Incohate Intellect, despairing of its separation from the One, attempts to reunite with it. And so, in an act of reversion, it turns and looks and sees only itself, thus constituting itself as the Intellect, a fragmentary image of the hyperontic One that contains within itself all that is intelligible. In the narrative, procession precedes reversion. But the narrative, here, is clearly mythic. The relevant procession and reversion are co-eternal activities and do not occur in separate time. There may, of course, be differences between procession and reversion in the intelligible and sensible realms. But these differences must be specified and shown to sufficiently justify the claim that within the sensible realm, procession and reversion must occur in separate time. Indeed, as we shall see (section~\ref{sec:ps_simplicius}), Ps-Simplicius, in his own development of the reversion account, emphasizes just such a difference (\emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 187,38–188,3) but where the act of reversion and the perceptual activity that it reverts upon occur simulateneously.

Second, Ps-Philoponus' premises seem unduly strong. As presented, we only get to the conclusion that what is able to revert upon itself is eternal and incorporeal, if the act of reversion is separate in the strong sense of being wholly separate from the body. But perhaps reversion in the sensible realm is separate if not wholly separate \citep[158]{Bernard:1987hc}. If possible at all, awareness of perceptual activity by sight's reversion upon it could not be wholly separate from the body since sight's activity in seeing involves the activity of its corporeal instruments, the eyes. Again, as we shall see (section~\ref{sec:ps_simplicius}), Ps-Simplicius, in his own development of the reversion account of perceptual self-consciousness, maintians that sight does not wholy revert upon itself.

\section{Proclus} % (fold)
\label{sec:proclus}

Proclus participates in the alien linguistic tradition tradition that deploys \emph{epistrophein} in discussing perceiving that we see, whether or not Ps-Philoponus explicitlty has him in mind in this passage. Relevant, here, are \textsc{Propositions} 15 (\emph{All that is capable of reverting upon itself is incorporeal}) and 16 (\emph{All that is capable of reverting upon itself has an existence separable from all body}) of \emph{Elementatio Theologica}. There are differences, however. Whereas Ps-Philoponus represents reasoning from separateness to incorporeality, Proclus, in \textsc{Proposition} 16, represents separateness as a consequence of incorporeality. (Though distinct lines of reasoning, they may be consistent should separateness and incorporeality prove mutually entailing.)

Let us begin by considering Proclus' case for incorporeality:
\begin{quote}
	\textsc{Proposition} 15: \emph{All that is capable of reverting upon itself is incorporeal.} For it is not in that nature of any body to revert upon itself. That which reverts upon anything is conjoined with that upon which it reverts: hence it is evident that every part of a body reverted upon itself must be conjoined with every other part—since self-reversion is precisely the case in which the reverted subject and that upon which it has reverted become identical. But this is impossible for a body, and universally for any divisible substance: for the whole of a divisible substance cannot be conjoined with the whole of itself, because of the separation of its parts, which occupy different positions in space. It is not in the nature, then, of any body to revert upon itself so that the whole is reverted upon the whole. Thus if there is anything which is capable of reverting upon itself, it is incorporeal and without parts. (Proclus, \emph{Elementatio Theologica}; \citealt[19]{Dodds:1963ul})
\end{quote}

There is a lot going on in this interesting argument (for recent discussion see \citealt[chapter 7.4]{Coope:2020aa}). Proclus' reasoning may be regimented as follows:

\begin{enumerate}[(1)]
	\item \textsc{Conjunction}: That which reverts upon anything is conjoined with that upon which it reverts.
	\item \textsc{Unity}: In self-reversion, that which reverts and that upon which it reverts become one.
	\item \textsc{Whole}: In self-reversion, the whole reverts upon the whole in the sense that every part is conjoined with every other part.
	\item If a body or divisible being is capable of self-reversion, then the whole of the body or divisible being can be conjoined to the whole.
	\item If the whole of a body or divisible being is conjoined with the whole, then every part of the body or divisible being is conjoined with every other part.
	\item The spatial separation of its parts, however, prevents every part of the body or divisible being from being conjoined with every other part.
	\item It is not of the nature of a body or divisible being to revert upon itself so that the whole is reverted upon the whole.
	\item Thus if there is anything that is capable of reverting upon itself, it is incorporeal and without parts.
\end{enumerate}
The reasoning falls into two parts. The first part, (1)–(3), consists in general claims about the nature of reversion and self-reversion. The second part, (4)-(8), argues that these claims cannot be sustained in the case of bodies or divisible beings. Let us consider these in turn.

The first part consists in a general claim about the nature of reversion and two specific claims about the nature of self-reversion. 

The general claim about the nature of reversion is \textsc{Conjunction}: All reversion involves the conjunction of that which reverts and that upon which it reverts. \citet[19]{Dodds:1963ul} translates \emph{sunaptetai} as conjunction, but it typically means to unite. Presumably, Dodds does so to distinguish \textsc{Conjunction} from \textsc{Unity}. In effect, he is emphasizing that something can conjoin or unite with something short of becoming identical with it. Not all reversion is self-reversion. Thus Proclus maintains that all things revert upon that from which it proceeds (\emph{Elementatio Theologica} \textsc{Proposition} 31). So all things under the One revert upon a higher cause distinct from them. Such reversion is manifestly not self-reversion. 

\textsc{Unity} is the first of the claims specifically about self-reversion. It is unclear what, in fact, Proclus meant by it. Given that we are here concerend with self-reversion, it is a case of something reverting upon itself. So, in the case of self-reversion, that which reverts and that upon which it reverts are already numerically one. Despite the numerical identity of that which reverts and that upon which it reverts, self-reversion remains a kind of unification. \citet[203]{Dodds:1963ul} makes a suggestion: ``But the thought may be that self-knowledge is the limiting case where subject and object, in which all types of knowledge \emph{tend} to identity, actually `become' identical.'' In knowing, there is the knower and the known. Socrates emphasizes to Critias that, in a range of familiar cases, what is known is distinct from the knowledge of them (\emph{Charmides} 166a3–7). In the case of self-knowledge, however, knower and known are numerically one. But a distinction may still be marked. Aristotle insists that the healer and the healed are distinct, and so if the physician and patient are one, then they are so incidentally, a fact that he expresses by saying that the physician in healing himself does so by treating themself \emph{qua} other (\emph{Metaphysica} {\sbl Δ} 12 1019a15–18). Similarly, one might maintain that if knower and known are one, then in knowing oneself, the knower treats themself \emph{qua} other. That might be the case for higher-order knowledge, where the knowing takes a distinct psychic activity as its object, but reversion is meant to account for the reflexive case. Perhaps in self-reversion, this vestigial difference is finally overcome, 

\textsc{Whole} is the second of the claims specifically about self-reversion. When Proclus, in the first part of the argument, is making general claims about self-reversion, he speaks of parts: ``every part of a body reverted upon itself must be conjoined with every other part''. When, in the second part of the argument, Proclus argues that corporeal self-reversion is impossible, he speaks instead of wholes: ``the whole of a divisible substance cannot be conjoined with the whole of itself''. If the overall reasoning is valid, talk of parts and wholes must be coordinated in the way that \textsc{Whole} describes in our regimentation. As we shall see, \textsc{Whole} is the lynchpin in the argument for incorporeality. Perhaps for this reason doubts about that argument tend to focus on \textsc{Whole} (see \citealt[521–2]{Barnes:2011kv} and \citealt[chapter 7.4]{Coope:2020aa}). 

Barnes is, frankly, incredulous:
\begin{quote}
	[T]he argument in ET 15\ldots turns not on thinking but on unification. `Whatever reverts to anything is conjoined with ({\sbl συνάπτεται}) what it reverts to.' Hence `self-reversion occurs when the two of them–that is, what reverts and what it reverts to–become one'. But `what has parts is not conjoined with itself as whole to whole because of the separation of the parts'. That is the argument, and it is hard to see merit in it: why should not x conjoin with x, `whole to whole', precisely insofar as each part of x conjoins with itself? Admittedly, the meaning of Proclus' words is obscure; but there are arguments whose invalidity is detectable even while their sense remains hidden–and such, I suspect, is the argument of ET 15. \citep[521–2]{Barnes:2011kv}
\end{quote}
Barnes' worry, though, is difficult to reconstruct. Barnes claims that the argument for \textsc{Proposition} 15 is invalid, but his sole complaint is about the soundness of the argument, having to do with the truth of \textsc{Whole}. ``[W]hy should not x conjoin with x, `whole to whole', precisely insofar as each part of x conjoins with itself?'' 

Moreover, despite the rhetorical question, it is hard to see the force of the worry. Two observations. First, substituting that claim:
\begin{quote}
	\textsc{Whole*}: In self-reversion, the whole reverts upon the whole in the sense that every part is conjoined with the whole
\end{quote}
with the claim that Proclus actually makes, \textsc{Whole}, would render his argument invalid. But that fact, by itself, does not make the argument actually given for \textsc{Proposition} 15 invalid. Second, there is something methodologically suspect about the query, ``[W]hy should not x conjoin with x, `whole to whole', precisely insofar as each part of x conjoins with itself?'' In a related vein, \citet[129–30]{Coope:2020aa} writes that ``[i]t is certainly not true in \emph{general} that if x (as a whole) {\sbl φ}-s y (as a whole), each part of x must {\sbl φ} every part of y.'' What Proculus means by the whole reverting upon the whole just is that each part of the whole is conjoined with every other part. It is technical language in a work of systematic philosophy from Late Antiquity. Why, then, engage in this ordinary language philosophy when trying to understand the extraordinary language of Proclus? (Not that there is anything wrong with ordinary language philosophy. It has its place, and I have engaged in it myself, but even Cook Wilson and Austin would deny that its methods are everywhere applicable.)

The claims specifically about self-reversion are the claims that cannot be sustained in the corporeal case. There are two of them, \textsc{Unity} and \textsc{Whole}. According to \textsc{Unity}, in self-reversion, that which reverts and that upon which it reverts are one. And according to \textsc{Whole}, in self-reversion, the whole reverts upon the whole in the sense that every part of the whole is conjoined with every other part. \textsc{Whole} is meant to be a consequence of \textsc{Conjunction}, insofar as it involves a claim about conjunction, and \textsc{Unity}. The fact that that which reverts and that upon which it reverts are conjoined and one entails that the whole reverts upon the whole. (Somehow. Though plausible, there must be a supressed premise since neither \textsc{Conjunction} nor \textsc{Unity} deploy mereological vocabulary.)

The incompatibility of \textsc{Whole} with the corporeal case is the centerpiece of the second half of the argument. The target question is whether bodies or other divisible beings may revert upon itself consistent with \textsc{Whole}. I take it that bodies are divisible beings, but not all divisible beings are bodies; otherwise, Proclus' claim that ``But this is impossible for a body, and universally for any divisible substance'' would be strangely redundant. \citet[203]{Dodds:1963ul} explains that ``[t]he intention is to exclude not only the material and corruptible body, but also (as is recognized in scholion preserved by PQ) the `first body', the {\sbl ὄχημα}'' (on the {\sbl ὄχημα} see \citealt[appendix 2]{Dodds:1963ul}). What makes a divisible being divisble is its posession of spatially separated parts into which it may be divided. Like David Lewis' Hume Highway, they exist partly here and partly there. It is this spatial separation of parts that is inconsistent with \textsc{Whole}. For a divisible being to revert upon itself, the whole must be conjoined with the whole in the sense that every part of the divisible being must be conjoined with every other part. But since the parts are spatially distributed, this is simply not possible. A spatially separated part may be conjoined with another spatially separated part but it won't be the case that every part is conjoined with every other part (think of the conjunction of parts of a folded towel).

Let us say that something wholly reverts if the whole reverts upon the whole. It is impossible for a body to wholly revert upon itself. For a body to wholly revert upon itself every part of the body must be conjoined with every other part. But since corporeal parts are spatially divided, it is impossible for the whole, in this way, to revert upon the whole. 

Augustine will exploit a similar idea, though its sources are more Porphyrean than Proclean. The greatness of the soul is manifest in the incoporeal soul's capacity to be wholly present to the spatially distributed parts of the body (\emph{De quantitate animae}) and its consequent explanation of apperceptive unity (\emph{De immortalitate animae} 16.25).

Allow me to make a brief remark about the conclusion of the passage. \textsc{Proposition} 15 itself only speaks of the incorporeality of that which reverts upon itself. But interestingly the conclusion of the demonstration is conjunctive: Everything that reverts upon itself ``is incorporeal and without parts''. Now, given that \textsc{Whole} is the lynchpin of the argument, it is perhaps not surprising that some mention is made of parts. But ``part'', here, must mean, specifically, spatial part, the kind of part suffered by divisible being. After all, the Plotinian second Hypostasis, Intellect, contains within itself all the intelligibles. Might not these reasonably be considered parts? They are, after all, elements of a unified manifold. But if they are, they are not spatially distributed parts.

Proclus's argument, if sound, establishes that a body may not wholly revert upon itself. What is at issue, however, in discussing whether, and to what extent, perceiving that we see and hear can be understood in terms of an act of reversion is whether sight, a perceptual power of a living being, reverts upon its characteristic activity, seeing. But despite the activity essentially involving the use of a corporeal instrument, neither the activity nor the power that issues it are themselves bodies. There is an opening, then, for Ps-Simplicius to exploit in defending a reversion account of perceiving that we see and hear.

\section{Ps-Simplicius} % (fold)
\label{sec:ps_simplicius}

Ps-Philoponus denies that perceiving that we see and hear is the activity of a perceptual power maintaining, instead, that it is the activity of the attentive part (\emph{tou prosektikou merous}) of reason (\emph{logos}) (\emph{In de anima}, 464,30–465,17). The attentive part is less a distinct faculty than an incidental ability that one has by possessing some other rational power. This a clear departure from the Peripatetic position that the power to perceive that we see and hear is perceptual. Against this, Ps-Simplicius will insist that while reason may play a role in reflexive awareness, it does so consistent with perceiving that we see and hear being the activity of a perceptual power that contrasts with rational powers (for a comparison of Ps-Philoponus and Ps-Simplicus see \citealt{Lautner:2008id}). Like Priscian, Ps-Simplicius (possibly one and the same) adheres to the Iamblichean doctrine that reason permeates all things (\emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 173,1–7, 187,35–9; on Priscian's adherence see \citealt[448]{Kalderon:2017rw}, on Ps-Simplicius' see \citealt[332]{Lautner:2014cz}; the language, here, \emph{diēkon}, is Stoic, originally one kind of body permeating another in a corporeal mixture, \citealt[125]{Blumenthal:2000rf}). Reason permeates even the perceptual powers of the perceiver. But that is consistent with the perceptual powers, so permeated, and not reason, explaining that we perceive that we see and hear.

While Ps-Simplicius seeks to find a role for reason in explaining perceptual self-awareness consistent with the claim that this is the activity of a perceptual power, there remains a striking divergence from the Peripatetic position. Whereas Aristotle maintains that reflexivity is a distinctive feature of animal life (\emph{Physica} 7.2 244b15), Ps-Simplicius maintains that reflexivity is a feature of, specifically, human life:
\begin{quote}
	On the other hand perceiving that we perceive seems to me to be a special characteristic of humans only. (Ps-Simplicius, \emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 187,28–9; \citealt[40]{Blumenthal:2000rf})
\end{quote}
Whereas human perception is reflexive, we may perceive that we see and hear, not so for animals, that while perceivers, are not equipped with reason. Thus for Ps-Simplicius, the perceptual powers of humans and non-human animals differ in kind. In this regard, Ps-Simplicius' position anticipates McDowell's \citeyearpar[49–50, 63–5, 69–70, 114–23, 182–4]{McDowell:1994am}: ``Creatures without conceptual capacities lack self-consciousness\ldots'' \citet[114]{McDowell:1994am}. However, Ps-Simplicius endorses a particularly strong version of this claim that he attributes to Iamblichus: The power of perception in humans is homonymous with the power of perception in nonrational animals (\emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 187,35–9, see also 173,1–7; \citealt[109]{Lautner:1994cs}).

Another departure from Aristotle is what Ps-Simplicius assumes in his explanation of the role of reason in perceptual self-awareness. Specifically, Ps-Simplicius assumes that higher causes influence lower causes given their relative position in the ontological hierarchy—the higher the position, the greater the extent of its influence (compare Proclus, \emph{Elementatio Theologica} \textsc{Propositions} 56, 60, \citealt[125 n113]{Blumenthal:2000rf}). 

In what sense does reason permeate perception consistent with the contrast between perception and reason? Reason permeates all things at least in the sense that all things aspire to imitate reason insofar as they can. So our perceptual powers, bound as they are to their corporeal instruments, are limited in the extent to which they may imitate the operation of rational powers. Though limited, they are not utterly impotent in this regard. While reason, being incorporeal, may wholly revert upon itself, perception, being partially corporeal, may only partially revert upon itself. 

Perception is partially corproreal since only animate bodies perceive. The animal only has a special sense if it possesses the relevant corporeal instrument. However, not only must this corporeal instrument be appropriately juxtaposed to the rest of the animal's body, but it must be (1) animated by \emph{psuchē} (incorporeal, by Ps-Simplicius' lights) and (2) integrated into the vital operation of the whole and healthy animal. Given these features over and above the juxtaposition of the corporeal instrument, the exercise of sight in seeing is a psychic activity, an activity of an animate as opposed to an inanimate body. And should the animal be rational, like human animals, then the power that operates the corporeal instrument will itself be suffused with reason. (On the contrast between the causal powers of animate and inanimate bodies see Aristotle, \emph{Physica} 7.2. 244b13–15.)

Not only is perception partially corporeal, but, as a consequence, it may only partially revert upon itself.

Perceiving that we see and hear is explained in terms of self-reversion:
\begin{quote}
	\ldots in a sense what is perceiving cognizes itself, when it cognizes itself while it perceives. And for this reason it reverts upon itself (\emph{epistrophein}) and belongs to itself\ldots (Ps-Simplicius \emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 187,33–4; \citealt[40]{Blumenthal:2000rf}, modified)
\end{quote}
In perceiving that we see and hear, the human animal cognizes itself, and what explains this is that perception reverts upon itself and so belongs to itself. So Ps-Simplicius subscribes to the kind of reversion account that Ps-Philoponus criticizes (\emph{In De
anima} 466,18-27). Ps-Simplicius even connects self-reversion with separability from the body. As the passage continues:
\begin{quote}
	\ldots and its being separate from bodies already shows through more, in so far as every body having different parts of itself in different places would never be able to turn its attention to itself. (Ps-Simplicius \emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 187,34–5; \citealt[40]{Blumenthal:2000rf})
\end{quote}
Like Ps-Philoponus (\emph{In De anima} 466,18-27) and Proclus (\emph{Elementatio Theologica} \textsc{Prop\-osition} 16), Ps-Simplicius connects self-reversion with separability \citep[125]{Blumenthal:2000rf}. And like Proclus (\emph{Elementatio Theologica} \textsc{Proposition} 16) but unlike Ps-Philoponus, Ps-Simplicius understands separability to follow on from incoporeality, given the obstacle to self-reversion posed by the spatial separation of the parts of a body (see also \emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 173,4–5 ``because body, on account of being with different parts of itself in different places, does not turn back on itself''; \citealt[22]{Blumenthal:2000rf}). How, then, can Ps-Simplicius' account avoid Ps-Philoponus' criticism? The problem can seem acute.

However, Ps-Simplicius' account is not the reversion account targetted by Ps-Philoponus. 

Notice, first, that it is not phenomenologically inapt in the way that Ps-Phi\-lo\-po\-nus' target is. On that account, first one sees, and only then does sight know that it sees in a subsequent act of reversion. But Ps-Simplicius maintians that perception cognizes itself while it perceives and so in non-separate time. The awareness that one sees and hears unfolds with perception's disclosure of its object over time.

More germane is the fact that in perceiving that we see, sight does not wholly revert upon itself:
\begin{quote}
	For it is not able to cognize is being or its power, nor on the whole is it stimulated by itself: it is cognitive only of its activity, and at the time when it is active. (Ps-Simplicius, \emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 188,1–2; \citealt[40]{Blumenthal:2000rf}, modified)
\end{quote}
Sight, in perceiving that it sees, does not wholly revert upon itself. Sight reverts, (1) neither upon its being, (2) nor power, (3) nor is the reversion self-stimulated—sight only reverts upon its activity, seeing, and while it yet sees. Let us consider these three denials in turn.

(1) Sight, in perceiving that it sees, does not revert upon its being. A scribe catching a glimpse of Proclus performing a theurgic rite is aware from within that he sees what he does. But the scribe, just by that fact, is not yet in a position to give an account of what it is to be sighted. (If the being of sight is apprehended in an act of self-reversion, such an act would put one in a position to give an account of the being of sight, even if discursively unfolding the being of sight in an account would require further reasoning.) So if the scribe's reflexive awareness is explained in terms of the self-reversion of their sight, this does not involve sight reverting upon its being—they are not thereby aware of what it is to be sighted. 

(2) Sight, in perceiving that it sees, does not revert upon its power. Seeing Proclus may be the realization and fulfillment of the scribe's sight, but so seeing does not exhaust that perceptual power. The sight of Proclus may be the expression of this power, but the power is not exhausted by this expression (\citealt[210]{Gadamer:1960st}, \citeyear{Gadamer:1966ai}). The scribe's sight, after all, remains capable of other visual acts. In perceiving that they see, the scribe is aware of what is seen as they are seeing it. The scribe is aware of the content of the occurent visual act. They are not thereby aware of the contents of other seeings, enjoyed on other occasions, let alone the contents of all possible seeings afforded them by sight. So if the scribe's reflexive awareness is explained in terms of the self-reversion of their sight, this does not involve sight reverting upon its power—they are not thereby aware of the power of sight, the source that overflows its expression in seeing.

(3) Moreover, sight, in perceiving that it sees by reverting only upon its activity, is not, on the whole, stimulated by itself. Perception is moved by its object. So Proclus moves the scribe's sight of him. But as Aristotle insists, what moves is prior in nature to that which is moved (\emph{Metaphysica} {\sbl Γ} 1010b35-6). Perception is more active for Ps-Simplicius than it is for Aristotle, and the object of perception is perhaps limited to being a \emph{causa occasionalis} (\citealt{Lautner:1994cs}, on Prisican, possibly one and the same, see \citealt{Kalderon:2017rw}). But the object of perception must still occasion that perception. So the scribe's sight reverting upon their seeing will depend upon Proclus moving their sense of vision. So unlike reason reverting upon itself, perceptual self-reversion is not wholly spontaneous inheriting, as it does, the reactive character of perception itself.

So the human animal, in being endowed with sight, permeated with reason, may perceive that they see, but only by reverting upon that activity as it unfolds over time. So the scribe's seeing Proclus and their perceiving that they see Proclus occur in non-separate time. And the scribe's perceiving that they see Proclus is due to their sight's reversion upon its activity. So the scribe's seeing Proclus and their sight reverting upon that activity occur in non-separate time. The act of reversion by which the scribe perceives that they see unfolds with their act of seeing Proclus' performance of the theurgic rite. 

Indeed, Ps-Simplicius connects the simultaneity of seeing and perceiving that we see with the latter not be the exercise of a distinct power (\emph{In libros Aristotelis De anima commentaria} 188,3–7; \citealt[111]{Lautner:1994cs}). The contrast with the higher-order alternative, where perceiving that we see and hear is due to some other \emph{aesthēsis}, is underscored by Ps-Simplicius use of \emph{sunaisthēsis}. In perceiving that we see, seeing and its object are jointly perceived. It is the same activity, seeing, that apprehends its object and its seeing of it.
